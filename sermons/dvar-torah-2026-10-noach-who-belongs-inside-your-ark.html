Who Belongs Inside Your Ark?

Rabbi Phillip Blue

← Back to D’var Torah archive

When does someone else’s problem become my responsibility?

Parashat Noach begins with a world in crisis. God tells Noach that the earth has become corrupt and filled with violence, and that destruction is coming. Yet Noach does something surprising: he does not argue. He does not plead for the innocent. He does not ask God to reconsider.

Instead, God tells Noach to build an ark. And Noach builds the ark.

We might be tempted to see Noach’s silence as a failure. But the Torah itself calls him “a righteous man” (Genesis 6:9). At the end of the chapter, it tells us that Noach did everything God commanded him (Genesis 6:22).

Perhaps we ought to take the Torah at its word.

Noach’s assignment was not to save the entire world. It was to build the ark and protect the lives entrusted to his care. That was no small responsibility.

We all have arks to build. They include our families, our relationships, our homes, and our communities. They include children who need us, parents who depend on us, and commitments that require our attention even when the wider world seems to be calling us elsewhere.

Sometimes keeping our own ark afloat is already difficult enough.

But Noach cannot be the end of the story. The Torah offers another model in Abraham.

When Abraham learns that Sodom faces destruction, he challenges God: “Shall the Judge of all the earth not do justice?” (Genesis 18:25). Sodom is not Abraham’s family or community. Its people are hardly presented as exemplary neighbors. Yet Abraham cares enough to speak on their behalf.

That impulse extends well beyond the biblical narrative. Rabbi Abraham Joshua Heschel, marching alongside Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. at Selma, demonstrated that concern for justice can—and must—reach beyond the boundaries of one’s own community.

Abraham reminds us that compassion cannot end at the walls of our ark.

Then there is Moses.

After the sin of the Golden Calf, God threatens to destroy Israel. Moses pleads for the people who have repeatedly challenged his leadership. He goes so far as to say, “If not, erase me, please, from the book that You have written” (Exodus 32:32).

Abraham argues for those outside his community. Moses is willing to put himself on the line for those already inside it.

That distinction matters.

We encounter worthy causes everywhere. We see injustice, suffering, and people who deserve help. But believing that a cause is just is not the same as accepting personal responsibility for carrying it. Our time, energy, and resources are finite. Compassion may extend further than our capacity to act.

And some obligations are not simply causes we choose. We belong to families and communities that already have a claim on us. For Jews, that includes Am Yisrael, the Jewish people. Responsibility for one another is part of belonging.

Does that mean we ignore everyone else? Certainly not. Abraham would never permit that. Does it mean we take on every problem in the world? Noach reminds us that even an ark has dimensions.

Perhaps that is why the name Israel is so fitting. Jacob receives that name after wrestling through the night. Of all the qualities for which a people might be named, ours is wrestling. We are not promised simple answers about where responsibility begins and ends.

Rabbi Tarfon captures the tension in Pirkei Avot 2:16: “It is not your responsibility to finish the work, but neither are you free to abandon it.”

We cannot shut the doors of our ark and pretend the world outside does not exist. But neither can we bring the entire world aboard.

So we return to Noach, not merely with criticism of his silence, but with a renewed appreciation for what he accomplished. He protected what God had entrusted to him.

Abraham teaches us to care beyond our ark. Moses teaches us to remember those already inside it. Jacob teaches us to wrestle with the boundaries of our obligations.

And Noach reminds us that, while we wrestle with those boundaries, there is still an ark to build.

What is the ark entrusted to me? Who depends on me to keep it afloat? When must I look beyond its walls, and when must I recognize the limits of what I can carry?

Sometimes the most faithful answer is also the most difficult:

This is the ark I have been given to build.
