With Great Power Comes Great Responsibility

PARASHAT LECH LECHA · OCTOBER 23, 2026

Dear CTI Family,

What if I told you that the Israelites' enslavement in Egypt began not with Pharaoh's cruelty, but with Abram's failure to protect his own wife?

It sounds like an extraordinary accusation against the father of the Jewish people. But it comes from Nachmanides, the Ramban, one of Judaism's greatest biblical commentators.

In this week's parasha, a famine forces Abram and Sarai to seek refuge in Egypt. Fearing that the Egyptians will kill him to take his beautiful wife, Abram asks Sarai to pretend she is his sister.

His reasoning is troubling: “So that it may go well with me because of you” (Genesis 12:13).

And indeed, things go very well for Abram. Pharaoh takes Sarai into his household, while Abram receives livestock, servants, and considerable wealth.

The plan works. For Abram.

Ramban does not excuse his behavior. Commenting on Genesis 12:10, he declares that “Abraham our father committed a great sin inadvertently.” He criticizes Abram for endangering Sarai and failing to trust in God's protection. Ramban even connects Abram's actions to the later enslavement of his descendants in Egypt.

But beyond Ramban's interpretation lies a fundamental moral question:

What happens when someone entrusted with protecting another person instead uses that relationship to protect himself?

Centuries later, King David commits a far more terrible offense.

David sleeps with Bathsheba, the wife of Uriah, one of his loyal warriors. When she becomes pregnant, David attempts to conceal his actions by bringing Uriah home from battle.

But Uriah refuses to enjoy the comforts of home while his fellow soldiers remain in the field. When David's plan fails, he arranges for Uriah to be abandoned in battle, where he is killed.

The contrast is extraordinary. Uriah refuses to abandon his responsibilities to his fellow soldiers, while David abandons his responsibility to Uriah.

Abram's actions and David's are certainly not equivalent. Abram exposed Sarai to danger; David deliberately arranged a man's death. Yet both misused their positions of authority to protect themselves at someone else's expense.

Jewish tradition offers a different understanding of responsibility.

Maimonides teaches that a husband should “honor his wife more than himself and love her as himself” (Mishneh Torah, Hilchot Ishut 15:19).

And when the Israelites demand a king, the prophet Samuel warns that a ruler may use his authority to take from the very people he is supposed to protect (I Samuel 8).

The message is clear: Authority creates an obligation to protect others, not a privilege to serve ourselves.

What makes these biblical stories remarkable is that the Torah does not conceal the failures of its greatest figures.

Abram could be the father of the Jewish people and still fail to protect his wife. David could be Israel's greatest king and still commit a terrible abuse of power.

Both things can be true.

Their failures do not erase their greatness. But their greatness does not excuse their failures.

Perhaps that is why these stories have been preserved. They remind us that whether we are parents, spouses, teachers, employers, or community leaders, our responsibility to others grows with the authority we possess.

God promised Abram that he would become a blessing. But being a blessing means more than receiving God's favor. It means becoming a source of blessing for those entrusted to our care.

And so, to quote the greatest rabbinical superhero of them all—Spider-Man:

“With great power comes great responsibility.”

Buen Shabbat,

Rabbi Phillip Blue
Congregation Tifereth Israel
