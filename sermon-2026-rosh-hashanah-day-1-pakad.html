Pakad: From Remembering to Showing Up

Rosh Hashanah Day 1, 2026 · Rabbi Phillip Blue

← Back to Torah & Sermons

וַה׳ פָּקַד אֶת־שָׂרָה כַּאֲשֶׁר אָמָר וַיַּעַשׂ ה׳ לְשָׂרָה כַּאֲשֶׁר דִּבֵּר

V'Adonai pakad et Sarah ka'asher amar, vaya'as Adonai l'Sarah ka'asher diber. And in the translation of Reverend Dr. David de Sola Pool in the machzor in front of us:

“Then the Lord visited Sarah as He had said, and the Lord did to Sarah as He had spoken.”

I want to focus on one word:

פָּקַד — pakad.

Reverend Dr. de Sola Pool translates it as “visited.” But what does that mean? God “visited” Sarah? Did God just drop by with some Dunks? You know, an iced regular and a couple of Munchkins? Obviously, that's not the kind of “visit” the Torah has in mind. The problem is that “visited” is trying to convey something in the Hebrew that doesn't fit neatly into English. And what really caught my attention was the rest of the verse.

“The Lord visited Sarah as He had said…”

And then:

“…the Lord did to Sarah as He had spoken.”

God said. God pakad. God did. And Sarah had been waiting a very long time. I mean, we're talking decades. God had promised Abraham and Sarah a child. Years passed. Sarah grew older. She doubted. She laughed. At one point she tried—and failed—to solve the problem herself. And yet, still she waited. So this is certainly not some kind of divine vending machine. You don't put enough quarters into the universe, press B7, and eventually God dispenses what you ordered. That's not how pakad works.

And it's not quite “godly karma” either—the idea that if you're good, good things will happen to you, and if you're bad, eventually the universe will make you pay. We know life doesn't work that way. Sarah certainly knew life doesn't work that way. Pakad seems to be saying something different. Our lives are not invisible to God. What happens matters. What we do matters. What is done to us matters. Promises matter. And there comes a time when what has been noticed must be attended to. So I started wondering: What does this word pakad actually mean?

And as is so often the case with Hebrew, the answer isn't quite as simple as opening a dictionary and finding an English equivalent. Because once you start following this little three-letter root—

פ־ק־ד — peh, kuf, dalet—

you discover that it shows up in some surprisingly familiar places. And those other words may help us understand what the Torah is telling us about Sarah— and what Rosh Hashanah may be asking of us.

What Does Pakad Actually Mean?

So where else do we find this root? If you've spent any time in Israel—or around Israelis—you may already know some of its relatives. Take the word:

מִפְקָד — mifkad.

A mifkad can be a roll call, a muster, even a lineup for inspection. You show up. Who's here? Who's missing? Is everybody where they're supposed to be? You are accounted for. Then there's:

פִּקָּדוֹן — pikadon.

A pikadon can be a bank deposit. And לְהַפְקִיד — l'hafkid is what you do when you deposit money in the bank. Then there's:

מְפַקֵּד — me-fa-ked.

A commander. And פַּקָּד — pakad is a rank in the Israeli police. Now, let's be clear: These words do not mean the same thing. They're not even particularly close. A bank deposit is not a police officer. A police officer is not a military roll call. And none of those things sound very much like God “visiting” Sarah. And yet— they all come from the same three Hebrew letters:

פ־ק־ד — peh, kuf, dalet.

So I started wondering: What in the world do all of these things have to do with each other? I'm not suggesting that pakad has one secret English definition that somehow makes every one of these words mean the same thing. It doesn't. But maybe there is an idea running underneath them. A mifkad: people show up and are accounted for. A pikadon: something is placed in your care—and now you have to show up for that responsibility. A mefaked: someone is responsible for other people—and part of responsibility is showing up for the people in your charge.

And biblical pakad: someone or something receives a kind of attention that matters. Different words. Very different meanings. But underneath them, perhaps we can begin to hear: Attention. Accounting. Responsibility. And maybe one more: Showing up. And that phrase—showing up—started to change the way I heard the opening of our Torah reading. Maybe when God pakad Sarah, the Torah isn't simply telling us that God remembered her. God showed up for her. After all those years of waiting— God showed up. God had made a promise. God took account of that promise. And then God acted. God said. God pakad. God did.

And maybe that's the difference we're beginning to uncover. Remembering someone is something you can do in your head. Showing up requires you to do something.

What Does It Mean to Be Seen?

And maybe that's why Sarah's story belongs on Rosh Hashanah. Because Sarah isn't the only woman we meet this morning who has spent years waiting. In our Haftarah, we meet Hannah. Hannah, too, desperately wants a child. Hannah, too, waits. She prays. She cries. And when Eli, the priest—the top religious leader of his generation—sees her praying, he thinks she's drunk. Think about that. Hannah is standing there pouring out her heart— and the person who is supposed to understand her completely misreads her. He sees her. But he doesn't really see her.

Meanwhile, she watches other people receive the very thing she wants most. And she has no idea whether her life is ever going to change. Sarah knows that feeling. Hannah knows that feeling. And I suspect most of us, in one way or another, know that feeling too. Some of us have waited for a child. Others haven't. But we've all waited for something. For an illness to get better. For a relationship to heal. For a job to come through. For someone we love to find their way. For an answer that never seems to come. And sometimes we've waited so long that we've wondered: Does anybody even see what I'm going through?

That's what makes the connection between Sarah and Hannah so striking. Our Torah reading begins:

וַה׳ פָּקַד אֶת־שָׂרָה

God pakad Sarah. And just beyond the end of this morning's Haftarah, the Bible uses the very same root about Hannah:

כִּי־פָקַד ה׳ אֶת־חַנָּה

Ki pakad Adonai et Hannah. God pakad Hannah. Two women who waited. Two women who wondered. Two women whose lives, ultimately, were not invisible to God. And maybe that's one reason these stories speak so powerfully on Rosh Hashanah. Because today is:

יוֹם הַזִּכָּרוֹן — Yom HaZikaron.

The Day of Remembrance. We ask God to remember us. But surely we're not worried that God has misplaced our file. We're asking for something more than that. See me. See what I've been carrying. See what I've done. See what I've failed to do. See what has been done to me. See the promises I've kept and the promises I've broken. Take account of me. All of me. And here's where another expression from the Bible caught my attention. Isaiah speaks of:

יוֹם פְּקֻדָּה — Yom Pekudah.

A day of reckoning. A day of accounting. You can hear our root sitting right there: Pekudah. Pakad. Peh, kuf, dalet. And there's another way of hearing that phrase that I find especially interesting: A day when you show up and give account. Well... we showed up. Here we are. Rosh Hashanah. And maybe now comes the harder part: What account do we give?

Taking Account

And that's where this gets a little uncomfortable. Because we can't spend Rosh Hashanah asking God: See me. Take account of me. Don't let my life be invisible. —and never ask whether we're doing the same for the people around us. Remember Hannah. Eli saw her. He was looking right at her. And he got her completely wrong. He saw a woman behaving strangely. He saw someone he thought was drunk. What he didn't see was a woman in pain. He saw her without really seeing her. And we do that too. We see people every day. We pass them in the hallway. We sit across from them at dinner. We work beside them.

We pray beside them. We may even live with them. But do we really take account of them? Do we know what they're carrying? Do we notice when something has changed? Do we stop long enough to ask? And when we do notice— do we show up? Because that's the thread we've been following through pakad. Attention. Accounting. Responsibility. Showing up. And if we're going to take account of the people around us, Rosh Hashanah also asks us to do something even harder: Take account of ourselves. Or, to put that into considerably less elegant English: Sometimes you have to check yourself.

Once a year, maybe we need our own mifkad. Our own roll call. Our own inspection. Who's here? Who am I right now? Who have I become over the past year? Where did I show up? Where didn't I? What did I get right? Where did I fall short? What promise did I keep? What promise am I still making excuses about? Who did I see? And who did I look right past? That's an accounting. And an honest accounting isn't about beating ourselves up. If you're going to take account, you have to take account of everything. The kindness you showed when nobody noticed. The responsibility you took on. The person you helped.

The promise you kept. Put those on the scale too. But then— the hurt you caused. The responsibility you avoided. The person you judged too quickly. The promise you didn't keep. Those go on the scale as well. Everything counts. Because if we're asking God to take account of all of us, then we have to be willing to look at all of ourselves.

From Remembering to Doing

And then comes the part that I think pakad demands. We have to do something about what we find. Remember how our Torah reading began: God said. God pakad. God did. Not: God noticed. God thought about it. God made a mental note to circle back sometime after the holidays. God did. And if you'll permit me, I'd like to do something very Jewish with language. You know how we use the word schlep in English because it just does a job that English doesn't quite do? Today, I'd like to take this Hebrew word—pakad—and turn it into an English verb.

So I want to ask each of us to do one thing before this Rosh Hashanah is over. Think of one person you've been meaning to reach out to. You probably already know who it is. Someone you need to thank. Someone you need to apologize to. Someone who's been struggling and you've thought more than once: I really ought to check in. Or maybe there's no crisis at all. Maybe you simply haven't told them lately that they matter to you. You've remembered them. Now— pakad them. Show up for them. Let remembering become action. And maybe it isn't a person. Maybe it's a promise you've been meaning to keep.

A responsibility you've been avoiding. Something wrong you've noticed but haven't done anything about. You've remembered it. Pakad it. Isaiah asks:

וּמַה־תַּעֲשׂוּ לְיוֹם פְּקֻדָּה

U'mah ta'asu l'Yom Pekudah?

“What will you do on the day of accounting?”

What will you do? So choose one. One person. One promise. One responsibility. Pakad it. God said. God pakad. God did. And perhaps that's what we can try to do as well. We remember. We pakad. We act. And once you've pakaded someone—or maybe once you've pakaded yourself—you may discover that it's time to do something else. It may be time to do teshuvah. Because once we've really taken account—once we've seen what needs our attention—we sometimes discover that we need to change. To turn. To return. And that's where we'll pick up tomorrow:

תשובה — teshuvah.

Shanah tovah. Buen anyo i Muchos anyos.
