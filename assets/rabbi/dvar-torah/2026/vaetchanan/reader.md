Parashat Va'etchanan – Shabbat Nachamu

There is a profound difference between optimism and hope.

Optimism is the belief that things will probably work out. Hope is the determination to keep believing, building, and acting even when we don't know how things will turn out.

That distinction lies at the heart of Parashat Va'etchanan and Shabbat Nachamu.

Just a few days ago we sat on the floor on Tisha B'Av, reading Megillat Eichah, mourning the destruction of the Beit HaMikdash, and recalling centuries of Jewish suffering. We did not minimize our grief or rush past it. Judaism insists that pain be acknowledged honestly.

The opening words of Eichah capture that grief:

אֵיכָה יָשְׁבָה בָדָד הָעִיר רַבָּתִי עָם

Eichah yashvah badad ha'ir rabbati am.

"Alas! Lonely sits the city that was once so full of people." (Lamentations 1:1)

But then, almost without warning, the mood changes.

This Shabbat we read the first of the seven Haftarot of consolation. It begins with one of the most stirring proclamations in all of Tanach:

נַחֲמוּ נַחֲמוּ עַמִּי יֹאמַר אֱלֹהֵיכֶם

Nachamu, nachamu ami, yomar Eloheichem.

"Comfort, comfort My people," says your God. (Isaiah 40:1)

Notice what Isaiah does not say.

He does not explain why tragedy occurred.

He simply commands:

"Comfort My people."

Before there are answers, there is compassion. Before there are explanations, there is comfort. Before there is certainty, there is hope.

The same lesson appears in our parashah.

Moses opens with a heartfelt plea:

וָאֶתְחַנַּן אֶל־ה׳ בָּעֵת הַהִוא לֵאמֹר

Va'etchanan el Hashem ba'et hahi lemor.

"I pleaded with the Lord at that time…" (Deuteronomy 3:23)

The Midrash teaches that Moses prayed 515 times, because the numerical value (gematria) of וָאֶתְחַנַּן is 515.

Yet God's answer remained no.

Moses would see the Land of Israel, but he would not enter it.

Most of us, after hearing "no" once, might give up. Moses hears "no" after 515 heartfelt prayers—and yet he refuses to become bitter.

Instead, he spends the rest of his life teaching the Shema, repeating the Aseret HaDibrot, and preparing Joshua to lead. He invests in a future he himself will never experience.

That is hope.

Hope is continuing the mission even when your own dreams remain unfulfilled.

Rabbi Lord Jonathan Sacks beautifully captured this distinction: "Optimism is the belief that things will get better. Hope is the belief that together we can make things better."

Optimism waits for circumstances to improve.

Hope asks, "What can I do today to help bring redemption one step closer?"

The Lubavitcher Rebbe offered a beautiful insight into the opening words of this week's Haftarah.

He noted that God does not simply promise that comfort will come someday. The words "נַחֲמוּ נַחֲמוּ עַמִּי" are also a call to action. God is speaking to the prophets—and through them, to every generation of Jews—charging us to become His partners in bringing comfort to His people.

Hope is not sitting back and waiting for better days.

Hope is becoming God's partner in creating better days.

That means comforting someone who is grieving, visiting the sick, supporting Israel, performing acts of chesed, and reminding every Jew that they never stand alone.

That message is echoed in the central declaration of this week's parashah:

שְׁמַע יִשְׂרָאֵל ה׳ אֱלֹהֵינוּ ה׳ אֶחָד

Shema Yisrael, Hashem Eloheinu, Hashem Echad.

"Hear, O Israel: The Lord is our God, the Lord is One." (Deuteronomy 6:4)

The Shema is more than a declaration about God.

It is a reminder that we are one people. We may disagree. We may come from different communities and hold different opinions. But our covenant is greater than our differences, and our shared destiny is greater than our disagreements.

I think that is exactly the message we need today.

The Jewish people are living through extraordinarily difficult times. Israel still bears the wounds of war, while Jews around the world face a renewed wave of antisemitism. This is not a time for naïve optimism. It is a time for resilient hope.

In English, hope is often described as a feeling. In Judaism, hope is a mitzvah.

It is the choice to continue living Jewishly—to pray, to celebrate Shabbat, to teach Torah, to support Israel, to perform acts of chesed, and to strengthen our communities—not because the world is already redeemed, but because we believe our actions help bring redemption closer.

That is Moses' message in Va'etchanan.

That is Isaiah's message in Nachamu.

That is Rabbi Lord Jonathan Sacks' distinction between optimism and hope.

And that is the Lubavitcher Rebbe's challenge: not simply to wait for comfort, but to become comforters ourselves.

So as we begin these seven weeks of consolation leading toward Rosh Hashanah, may we hear Isaiah's words not only as a promise, but as a calling:

נַחֲמוּ נַחֲמוּ עַמִּי

Nachamu, nachamu ami.

"Comfort, comfort My people."

May we become that comfort for one another, and may we soon merit to see fulfilled the prophet's closing promise:

וְקוֹיֵי ה׳ יַחֲלִיפוּ כֹחַ יַעֲלוּ אֵבֶר כַּנְּשָׁרִים יָרוּצוּ וְלֹא יִיגָעוּ יֵלְכוּ וְלֹא יִיעָפוּ

V'kovei Hashem yachalifu koach; ya'alu eiver kanesharim; yarutzu v'lo yiga'u; yeilchu v'lo yi'afu.

"But those who hope in the Lord shall renew their strength. They shall soar on wings like eagles; they shall run and not grow weary; they shall walk and not faint." (Isaiah 40:31)

May the Holy One comfort all who mourn, grant healing to the wounded, protect the soldiers and security forces who defend the State of Israel, bless Israel with security, wisdom, and peace, and inspire us to become bearers of hope for the entire Jewish people.

Shabbat Shalom.
