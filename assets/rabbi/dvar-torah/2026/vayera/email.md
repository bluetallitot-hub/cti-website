Abraham is ninety-nine years old. He has just undergone circumcision. And according to rabbinic tradition, it is the third day after the procedure, when the pain is particularly intense.

You would think he might appreciate a little peace and quiet.

But when three strangers appear near his tent, Abraham runs to greet them. He offers water, shade, and something to eat. Then he involves Sarah and the rest of his household in preparing a meal.

We usually understand this story as an example of *hachnasat orchim*, the mitzvah of welcoming guests.

But I think something more is happening.

**Abraham isn't simply practicing hospitality. He's teaching justice.**

A few verses later, God explains why Abraham was chosen:

**כִּי יְדַעְתִּיו לְמַעַן אֲשֶׁר יְצַוֶּה אֶת־בָּנָיו וְאֶת־בֵּיתוֹ אַחֲרָיו**

“For I have chosen him, so that he may instruct his children and his household after him.” (Genesis 18:19)

God doesn't simply say Abraham will be righteous. God says Abraham will **teach righteousness**, not only to his children but to his entire household.

And perhaps we've just witnessed how.

Abraham doesn't gather everyone for a lecture about welcoming strangers. He sees people who need hospitality, and his household gets to work.

It's much like our own Shabbat tables. Someone prepares the food, someone sets the table, and someone finds another chair. Our families learn what matters by participating in what we do.

The Torah describes Abraham's mission as practicing *tzedakah u'mishpat*—righteousness and justice.

We usually think of hospitality as *chesed*, loving-kindness. But justice suggests something more. **Kindness is something we choose to offer. Justice recognizes that another person has a claim upon us, whether or not it is convenient.**

And Abraham takes that principle seriously.

When God reveals the impending destruction of Sodom, Abraham challenges God:

**הֲשֹׁפֵט כָּל־הָאָרֶץ לֹא יַעֲשֶׂה מִשְׁפָּט**

“Shall the Judge of all the earth not do justice?” (Genesis 18:25)

Abraham isn't defending Sodom's wickedness. He's insisting that innocent people must not suffer simply because of where they live.

The man who welcomed strangers into his tent now demands justice for strangers he may never have met.

Then the Torah takes us to Sodom, where Abraham's nephew Lot is living.

When two strangers arrive, Lot welcomes them into his home, just as Abraham had welcomed his visitors.

The similarity is striking.

Lot had spent years in Abraham and Sarah's household. The Torah doesn't explicitly tell us that Abraham taught him hospitality, but perhaps Lot learned something simply by living there.

And now that lesson is being tested.

The people of Sodom surround Lot's house and demand that he surrender his guests. Lot attempts to protect them.

But listen to what the people of Sodom say when Lot objects:

“This one came here as a stranger, and now he presumes to judge!” (Genesis 19:9)

Notice the contrast.

**Abraham demands justice. The people of Sodom resent being judged.**

Perhaps Sodom's deepest failure isn't simply its refusal to welcome strangers. Its people have lost the ability to recognize why strangers deserve protection at all.

Lot remembers at least part of what he learned in Abraham's household.

And that brings us back to our own homes.

We can tell our children a hundred times that welcoming guests is a mitzvah. But when they grow up in a home where there's always room for one more person at the Shabbat table, perhaps we don't need to explain very much.

They already know.

We cannot control every environment our children will encounter or every choice they will make. But we can help shape the values they carry with them.

Because the hospitality we practice today may become the justice someone else defends tomorrow.
