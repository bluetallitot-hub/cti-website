Mechilah: What Happens Next?

Yom Kippur 2026 · Rabbi Phillip Blue

← Back to Torah & Sermons

So perhaps there is a progression to these High Holidays. First, pakad: I have to notice. Then, teshuvah: I have to respond. But there is still something missing. What happens after I notice that a relationship has been damaged? What happens after someone says, “I was wrong,” or “I’m sorry”? What happens next?

For that, we need another Hebrew word: מְחִילָה — mechilah. Forgiveness.

And mechilah may be the most difficult of the three. There are things in life that cannot be fixed, words that cannot be unsaid, betrayals that cannot simply be forgotten, and wounds that remain even after someone has apologized. So what does it mean to forgive when what happened cannot be undone?

Mechilah does not change the past. It doesn’t say that what happened was acceptable. It doesn’t require us to pretend that we weren’t hurt. So what does forgiveness do?

There is an extraordinary story from Israel that I think gives us an answer. It is the story of Yotam Haim, his mother Iris, and an unbearable mistake. And it teaches us something about mechilah:

Mechilah cannot change what happened. But it can change what happens next.

Yotam Haim

On October 7, 2023, Yotam Haim was 28 years old and living on Kibbutz Kfar Aza. Before I tell you what happened to Yotam, I want you to know a little sliver about who he was.

Yotam was a musician—a drummer in the heavy-metal band Persephore. He loved animals. He worked in restaurants. He played basketball. And his family has spoken very openly about the struggles he faced with his mental health. At his funeral, his mother Iris spoke about the courage it sometimes took simply for Yotam to get up every morning and face another day:

“We had so many conversations about your coping, your bravery, about your choice to get up every day and to keep living alongside the suffering that was so hard on you.”

Yotam dreamed of becoming a famous drummer. But Iris remembered another dream as well: “You spoke about a better world, you wanted a world that would be better, without evil and revenge.”

On October 7, Yotam was kidnapped from Kfar Aza and taken into Gaza. For more than two months, Iris refused to give up hope. She believed her son was alive and strong enough to survive.

And remarkably, he did.

Yotam, together with two other hostages, Alon Shamriz and Samer Talalka, escaped from Hamas captivity. They had survived October 7. They had survived more than two months in captivity. And somehow, they escaped.

They were free. But they were still inside Gaza.

On December 15, the three men encountered soldiers from the IDF’s 17th Battalion in Shejaiya. The soldiers mistook them for terrorists and opened fire. Alon and Samer were killed immediately. Yotam was shot as well, but somehow survived and escaped into a nearby building.

According to Iris’s account, an officer heard Yotam calling for help, realized that the voice sounded Israeli, and ordered the soldiers to stop firing. Yotam was called to come out.

And then came one final, almost unbearable tragedy.

Two soldiers apparently had not heard the order to stop firing. They fired. Yotam survived friendly fire once, only to be killed by friendly fire moments later.

Yotam Haim—the young man who had survived October 7, survived more than two months of Hamas captivity, escaped his captors, and even survived being mistakenly shot by his own army—was gone through another terrible mistake.

There are moments like this when we ask one of the oldest questions human beings have ever asked: Why do bad things happen to good people? Where was God? Why Yotam? Why, after everything he had already survived, did his story have to end this way?

I don’t have an answer to those questions. And I don’t think Iris did either.

But Iris faced another question—one only she could answer: What was she going to do now? What was she going to do with her grief and her anger? And what was she going to do about the soldiers whose terrible mistake had taken her son’s life?

Iris Haim and Mechilah

First, Iris grieved. She has never pretended that what happened was somehow all right. She describes the night she learned of Yotam’s death as one of the only moments in her life when she experienced true despair.

But two days later, her therapist asked her to imagine that Yotam were sitting in front of her, and then to speak as Yotam. Iris imagined her son saying: “I died as a free person.”

He had not died as a hostage. He had escaped Hamas. He had fought his way to freedom. Iris began calling him “Yotam the partisan.”

It did not change what had happened. Yotam was still gone. But Iris was beginning to decide what she was going to do with what had happened.

During the shiva, Iris received a message about the soldiers of the 17th Battalion. They were broken. These were soldiers who had gone into Gaza to defend Israel and help bring the hostages home. Instead, they had been involved in the deaths of three of the people they were supposed to save.

Iris was told, “These soldiers cannot continue fighting. They are broken.”

Think about the choice she had. She could have said: Good. They should feel guilty. They killed my son. No one could have blamed her.

Instead, Iris said: “I want to talk to them and to comfort them and to tell them that we are not angry.”

She recorded a message for the battalion. She told them that they had gone into Gaza to defend Am Yisrael. She told them that she did not blame them. She told them that they had to continue.

And she offered them מְחִילָה — mechilah. Forgiveness.

That was not the end of Iris’s relationship with the soldiers. They began calling her, coming to hear her speak, and asking to meet her. Iris described soldiers carrying enormous guilt—sometimes, she said, they seemed angrier with themselves than she was with them.

One soldier who had been nearby that day asked to meet Iris. For two months, she says, he could barely eat or sleep. They met at a restaurant in Tel Aviv, and for almost an hour he cried.

Think about that scene. A soldier sits across the table from a grieving mother, overwhelmed by guilt over what happened to her son. And who comforts whom?

Iris comforts him.

Eventually she asks what he does when he is not a soldier. He tells her he is a chef specializing in Italian food. Iris tells him Yotam loved to cook too and suggests that he create a special dish in Yotam’s memory.

Iris says something changed almost immediately. He stopped crying and began thinking about what he could do. Eventually, he returned to his work, the army, and his life.

That is the power of mechilah.

The Wound and the Scar

Iris could not change October 7 or December 15. She could not bring Yotam back or make the soldiers’ mistake disappear.

On Rosh Hashanah, I talked about wounds and scars. When you cut your arm, the wound eventually closes and the body becomes healthy again. But sometimes a scar remains.

The scar is not proof that healing failed. It is proof that healing happened.

Mechilah doesn’t make the scar disappear either. Forgiveness doesn’t mean looking at the scar and pretending there was never a wound. The wound was real. What happened to Yotam was real. Iris’s grief was real. And the terrible mistake of the 17th Battalion was real.

But Iris refused to keep ripping the wound open. Even more remarkably, she refused to allow that wound to destroy the people on the other side of it.

She gave those soldiers something they could not give themselves: permission to get back up, to continue, and to believe that the worst thing they had ever been part of did not have to define the rest of their lives.

Perhaps that is another way to understand mechilah.

Mechilah doesn’t remove the scar. It allows the wound to begin to heal.

And that may be the real power of forgiveness: Mechilah cannot change what happened. But it can change what happens next.

What Does Mechilah Ask of Us?

What Iris did was extraordinary. I am not sure I would have been as strong were I in her position. But mechilah does not belong only to extraordinary people in extraordinary circumstances. It is one of the central ideas of Yom Kippur.

Last night, in Ana B’Korenu, we repeatedly asked God, “Hear, O Lord. Forgive, O Lord.” Throughout the day, in the Viddui, we confess our own failures.

Judaism does not begin forgiveness by pretending nothing happened. It begins by acknowledging the wound.

The Mishnah teaches in Yoma 8:9 that for sins between a person and God, Yom Kippur can bring atonement. But for sins between one person and another, Yom Kippur does not bring atonement until we have made things right with the person we hurt.

In other words, there are things for which I can stand here all day asking God to forgive me, and God, in effect, says: That is not in My job description. You hurt another human being. You have to go to them.

Because God cannot give me mechilah on behalf of somebody I have hurt.

Our tradition recognizes that this can take time. Eventually there are two questions. For the person who caused the wound: Have I really done everything I can to make this right? And for the person carrying the wound: How much power am I going to allow this wound to have over what happens next?

That brings us back to the Haim family.

Iris answered that question one way. Her son Tuval answered it differently. When Iris said that the family was not angry with the soldiers, Tuval objected: “Why are you saying that I’m not angry? I am angry.”

Later he said: “I’m still not in the place of full forgiveness.”

I am glad Tuval said that. Otherwise, we might hear Iris’s story and come away with a terrible lesson: If Iris Haim could forgive the people involved in killing her son, surely you can forgive whatever somebody did to you.

No.

We don’t get to tell another person when they have to forgive. We don’t get to measure somebody else’s pain.

Iris was ready for mechilah. Tuval wasn’t.

But Tuval eventually said something else: “The anger will finish me.”

His anger wasn’t unfounded. His wound was real. But he recognized that at some point he had to decide how much of the rest of his life he was willing to give over to what had happened to him.

Forgiveness doesn’t mean pretending we weren’t wounded. It doesn’t mean calling something right that was wrong. It doesn’t mean forgetting. And it doesn’t mean that every broken relationship has to be restored. Some relationships are dangerous. Toxic even. Sometimes walking away is exactly what we need to do.

But sometimes there are words left unsaid. Sometimes there is a relationship we have written off that perhaps doesn’t need to be over.

And then the question becomes: How much power am I going to allow this wound to have over what happens next?

What Happens Next?

There is one final chapter to the story of Iris Haim and the 17th Battalion.

After Yotam was killed, the soldiers were devastated and struggled to continue fighting. Iris told them they had to get up. They had to continue. They still had a job to do.

And they did.

Ten months later, in October 2024, the 17th Battalion was fighting in Rafah when its soldiers encountered several Hamas terrorists. They fought and killed them. Only afterward did they discover that one of them was Yahya Sinwar—the architect of October 7.

I’m not telling you that because mechilah somehow made that happen. I tell you because of what the battalion told Iris afterward: her mechilah had given them the strength to get up after a terrible mistake and continue.

And Tuval? He was still angry, but not as angry as he had been. He hadn’t forgotten. The wound hadn’t disappeared. But something had changed for him too.

Mechilah did not change what happened. Yotam was still gone. Iris was still his grieving mother. Tuval was still his grieving brother. The wound was still there.

But what they did with that wound could change what happened next.

And maybe that is the question Yom Kippur leaves for us.

Perhaps there is someone we stopped speaking to. Someone whose phone number we still have but never call. A sibling. A parent. A child. A friend. Someone who hurt us. Someone we hurt. Someone we have simply shut out of our lives.

Maybe there is a good reason. Maybe it’s petty. Maybe it’s been so long we can’t even remember the reason.

Not every relationship should be restored. But maybe there is someone for whom the relationship does not have to be over. Someone we should be talking to instead of avoiding. Someone we should be listening to instead of shutting out. Someone we might even be ready to forgive.

And if there is, perhaps the question is not: Can I erase what happened?

We can’t.

Perhaps the question is:

What do I want to happen next?

Later this afternoon, we will begin Ne’ilah with El Nora Alilah, a piyyut that comes to us from the great poetry of medieval Sephardic Spain. When we sing it, listen for one word:

מְחִילָה — mechilah.

הַמְצִיא לָנוּ מְחִילָה בִּשְׁעַת הַנְּעִילָה

Hamtzi lanu mechilah, bish’at ha-ne’ilah. Grant us mechilah at the hour of Ne’ilah.

By then we will have spent an entire day asking God for forgiveness. But when you hear that word, perhaps think about one other person too.

The person from whom you need mechilah.

Or perhaps the person to whom you might be able to give it.

Because mechilah cannot change what happened. But it can change what happens next.

Taanit Liviano. Muchos Años.
