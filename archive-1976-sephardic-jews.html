After centuries of persecution, Peabody is home for Sephardic Jews

The Peabody Times · October 4, 1976 · Byline unknown · Page 3

Open full-size archival scan →

Primary source: Jewish Community of Peabody (Mass.) Records I-581, Box 1, Folder 15, image 033, Wyner Family Jewish Heritage Center.

PEABODY — In 1911, after 500 years of exile in Turkey, a small band of Jewish families migrated to America and re-established their close-knit, small Sephardic (Spanish) Jewish community in the Peabody-Salem area.

Today, the clan centers its religious and social life around their Temple, Congregation Tifereth Israel, which was founded in February, 1922. Once strictly Orthodox, the congregation is now more liberal, using English in addition to Spanish and Hebrew in its services.

The small, white clapboard building on Pierpont Street has a small Star of David on the front door, unpretentious but aristocratic -- just like the Sephardics.

The Temple is the heart of the community: its president and leader, Joseph Gibely, is a descendant of the original immigrants who settled here 65 years ago.

During the week preceding the High Holy Days, Gibely spent every night preparing the shul (temple) for the worship services; writing down the names of participants who would read from the prayer books; changing religious cloths to the holiday white; and arranging prayer books for the 100 congregants and their families.

Since the Temple has a moderate number of families (30 men), much of the work has to be done by the officers of the Temple and Gibely, as usual, devoted dozens of hours to work.

Gibely beams with pride whenever mention is made of the Temple; it is the center of his life, and he frequently speaks about it to area groups -- Jewish and non-Jewish. He loves to discuss the origins of his Sephardic community.

“Surprisingly enough, not many Jews know the history of oppression that the Sephardics suffered,” he said. Gibely interrupted the High Holy Days preparations to discuss his community.

“At my talks, some of the most frequently asked questions are, ‘Who are the Sephardic Jews?’ ‘Where did they come from?’ I answer these questions by giving a brief history of the Inquisition, and life in medieval Spain; two things which played such a vital role in the Sephardic Jewish life,” said Gibely.

In 14th century Spain, Jews often were forcibly converted, killed or driven out of the country. Many converted to escape persecution and death.

The ‘New Christians,’ as they were officially called, could legally trade, enter universities, practice law, enter government and medical fields.

But the populace distrusted them, and called the converts ‘Marranos,’ a derisive term that meant ‘swine.’

“Outwardly, the Marranos were Christians; inwardly, they were Jews. Their disbelief in the dogmas of the church was notorious. They kept traditional Jewish ceremonies; some would eat only kosher meat; usually they married exclusively among themselves; they consorted with ex-coreligionists and sometimes furtively attended synagogue. Worst of all, in the eyes of the church, they transmitted their disbelief to their children,” explained Gibely.

The New Christians entered every profession -- even the church. In time, they all but dominated Spanish life; within a few generations, almost every aristocratic family had some Jewish blood in it.

They held fiercely to their Jewish faith. Without schools, without books, without leadership, and in constant danger of being discovered, they held on.

Finally, in 1464, the church began inquiring into the conduct of the New Christians, after some converts had been found celebrating Passover.

“Several prisons were given the power to investigate heretics. By 1481, the first converts were tried, judged guilty of heresy, and burned alive. During the next 350 years, the Spanish Inquisition tried 400,000 subjects; 30,000 were put to death,” said Gibely.

“It wasn’t until 1834 that the institution of the Inquisition was finally abolished.”

In the meantime, those who had remained open, practicing Jews, also suffered in Spain and Portugal.

They were barely tolerated and often attacked and murdered in those two countries during the Middle Ages. In 1492 the entire Jewish community was expelled from Spain, at the decree of Queen Isabella and King Ferdinand. In 1497, the Jews were also expelled from Portugal.

“It was at this point in history that thousands of Jewish immigrants fled into all parts of the Ottoman Empire (including North Africa and Asia Minor) and also to Italy,” said Gibely.

Other Spanish refugees established communities in Germany, Denmark, Holland, England, Austria and Hungary.

The ancestors of the Peabody Sephardics “probably left Spain in the late 1400’s and settled in Constantinople, Turkey, where they remained for the next 500 years,” said Gibely.

Then in 1911 the Peabody ancestors decided to emigrate to America, starting in 1911 came Leon and Rebecca Eskenas, Myer and Regina Benater; then the Gibely, Eskenazi, Yonis, Haday, Leon, Havian Pernitchi, Savy, Fermon and Erera families. They formed the nucleus of the present community known as the Congregation Tifereth Israel.

Gibely has been active in the Temple for 30 years, and president since 1972. He is the editor-in-chief of all the congregation’s newsletter; and master-of-ceremonies at all social events held at the temple for many occasions. He and wife, Victoria, have two sons, Morris, a teacher in Beverly, and Jack, an employe of JEC Tanning Co.

Associated with Fermon Tanning Corporation for 27 years, he is also the shop steward, officer of the employee welfare fund, member of the board of Local 21 Leather Workers for 10 years, and “unofficial interpreter for hundreds of Spanish-speaking residents.”

Gibely, like all of the members of the Sephardic congregation, speaks and reads Spanish, though the style is a little different from modern Spanish.

“The Sephardic Jews took their language with them, and over the centuries, has continued to speak medieval Spanish. In Turkey, we added some Turkish expressions, so Ladino, our Spanish, is a strange blend of Spanish and Turkish.”

In all parts of the world, the Sephardics kept their Spanish idiom, as the language of speech and as a literary medium. In addition to Hebrew, Ladino is used at all services.

“Now, we use more English so everyone can understand the service,” said Gibely.

“When our parents lived in Turkey, they spoke five or six languages fluently because of the cosmopolitan makeup of the community.

“In America, though, the second congregation doesn’t speak quite as many languages.

Last July 26, Gibely was presented a plaque “acknowledging with great pride his many contributions and unselfish efforts on behalf of the congregation”; on it was also inscribed Gibely’s favorite expression, one which he invariably utters to everyone: “May you go from strength to strength.” This is Gibely’s philosophy, and, he feels, the philosophy which has enabled the Sephardics to survive through the hostile centuries.
