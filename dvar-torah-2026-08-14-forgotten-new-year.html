A Forgotten New Year and a Timeless Lesson

Rosh Chodesh Elul · Rabbi Phillip Blue

← Back to D’var Torah archive

Buena Tadrada! Y Buen mez!

Tonight we have the privilege of welcoming two gifts at once: Shabbat and the new month of Elul.

And that leads me to a question to begin this evening.

How many Jewish New Years are there?

Most people instinctively answer, "One...wait, there's more than one?"

Some might be able to name two—the Rosh Hashanah we celebrate on the first of Tishrei and Tu BiShvat, the New Year for the trees.

The very first Mishnah in Tractate Rosh Hashanah begins with the words: This is something that most Jews have never heard before.

The other two in case you are wondering are the first of Nisan, the New Year for kings and the festivals, which is why the Rosh Hashanah we commonly refer to is the first of the seventh month, Tishrei, and today the first of Elul, the New Year for tithing animals.

Of the four, this is probably the least familiar. There are no apples and honey, no shofar, and no seder of symbolic foods.

So why did our Sages call it a Rosh Hashanah?

Every year, a farmer gathered the newborn sheep and cattle and let them pass one by one beneath a rod.

One...

Two...

Three...

Until the tenth.

The Torah commands:

"Every tenth animal that passes beneath the shepherd's rod shall be holy to the Lord." (Leviticus 27:32)

Notice what the owner didn't do.

He didn't choose the weakest animal.

He didn't choose the strongest.

He didn't wait until he felt prosperous.

He simply counted faithfully, and every tenth animal belonged to God.

At first glance, this has very little to do with the High Holidays.

But I think it has everything to do with them.

While ma'aser behemah (what the counting process is called) isn't tzedakah, they share the same spiritual foundation.

Both teach us that before we ask, "What belongs to me?" we first recognize that everything ultimately belongs to God.

Tzedakah is more than charity.

It reminds us that our money, our possessions, our talents, and even our time are gifts entrusted to us by God. We are not owners; we are just caretakers.

Perhaps that is why Elul begins here.

Before we begin examining our souls, Judaism asks us to examine our blessings.

Before we ask God for another year, we are asked what we have done with the gifts He entrusted to us during the last one.

Then comes the beautiful reversal.

Tonight we remember a shepherd counting his flock.

But in just one month...

We become the flock.

The Mishnah teaches that on Rosh Hashanah all humanity passes before God one by one, like sheep before their shepherd.

Many know this image from the Ashkenazi prayer Unetaneh Tokef. Although that piyyut is not part of our liturgy, it beautifully reflects this much earlier teaching of the Mishnah.

Throughout the year, we count what God has entrusted to us.

On Rosh Hashanah...

God counts us.

That gives new meaning to the words we will soon recite:

"Repentance, prayer, and tzedakah temper the severity of the decree."

Teshuvah changes our relationship with our past.

Tefillah changes our relationship with God.

Tzedakah changes our relationship with God's blessings.

As we begin Elul tonight, this forgotten Rosh Hashanah quietly asks us two questions.

Before we ask God,

"Remember me,"

Elul asks,

"Whom have you remembered?"

Before we pray,

"Write me in the Book of Life,"

Elul asks,

"Whose life is better because God entrusted you with His blessings?"

If we spend this month answering those questions—with sincere teshuvah, heartfelt tefillah, and generous tzedakah—then when we stand before the Divine Shepherd on Rosh Hashanah, we will do so not only asking for blessing but having already become a blessing.

May this month of Elul inspire us to become faithful stewards of God's blessings.

Buen mez, Chodesh Tov, and may we all be written and sealed for a year of life, health, peace, and blessing.

Buen Shabbat. Shabbat Shalom.
