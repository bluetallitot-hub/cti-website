From Constantinople to Peabody: Congregation Tifereth Israel

By Gary Band · Jewish Journal Staff

View original newspaper scan →

Source: The Jewish Journal, January 19, 2001, p. 25, continued on p. 26. Preserved archival image I-581-01-15-034.

January 19, 2001

Gary Band · Jewish Journal Staff

Joseph Gibely II of Peabody embodies the past, present and future of the North Shore’s only European Sephardic synagogue, Congregation Tifereth Israel on Pierpont Street in Peabody. For the past 28 years, Gibely has served as president of this intimate and welcoming congregation which was incorporated February 15, 1922. Though the shul claims only a 40-family membership, its rich history, empowering rabbi, and long tradition of lay leadership, may well propel Tifereth Israel through another 80 years of existence.

Gibely, 80, fluent in Spanish and Ladino, a language spoken by Sephardic Jews in Turkey, the Balkans, north Africa and the Americas, has the energy and appearance of a man 20 years his junior. Calling the temple his “number one love” after his wife of 58 years, Gibely sees to his myriad responsibilities with pride and dedication, carrying on traditions begun nearly four score ago. His late father, Morris, along with his uncles, one of whom became the first Jewish policeman in Salem, emigrated from Turkey in 1911.

They worked at Fermon Tanning in Peabody and, after a year of extremely difficult work, earned enough to send for their families back in Turkey. Joseph’s late mother Sarah, sister Stella and other family members came over in 1912 on the passenger ship Carpathia, famous for rescuing passengers from the sunken Titanic as they waited in life boats or clung to pieces of debris in the frigid north Atlantic.

When all members of Gibely’s immediate and extended multi-lingual family resettled on the North Shore, they began conducting services in each other’s homes in the Castle Hill section of Salem. The Congregation was led by Gibely’s late uncle Leo for 30 years and the cantor was the late Joseph Havian, “whose voice and delivery thrilled all of us with his beautiful Sephardic-style selections.” They were then renting space in back of an Ashkenazi shul called Knesset Y’Israel Anshen S’fard in Peabody, which later merged with Congregation Sons of Israel. Afterwards, they worshipped on the second floor of a building on Boston Street before obtaining their present-day location.

According to Gibely, the structure which became Tifereth Israel, built in 1906, was once owned by the Church of the Nazarene from whom it was purchased by the Congregation in 1957. The now roomy open spaces which comprise the temple’s social hall and sanctuary were once cubicles designed for private lessons and individual study. “If you saw the interior when it was first purchased, you wouldn’t believe it was the same place,” Gibely said, looking around the sanctuary as if for the first time.

Though they didn’t have much money, the first Tifereth Israel congregants did have something very important. The early congregation, according to Gibely, had in its possession three Sepher Torahs that have remained in the arc to the present day. “Not even with all the combined salaries of the first members could they have afforded one, let alone three Torahs,” he said. “How they got them I have no idea.”

When they purchased the building, each man assessed himself at $100. They each paid $5 per week into a yukes, a sort of communal bank. “The courage and determination of a small Sephardic congregation to have a place they could call their own was incredible,” said Gibely. “But initially, to leave their homeland, to lift up roots and start a new life here, took more courage than anything.”

Tifereth Israel has indeed changed dramatically over the years, evolving both esthetically and spiritually, thanks to numerous contributions from congregants past and present. According to Gibely, although some families have moved on, others whose parents and grandparents were once members always come back for the High Holidays.

“They come from Framingham, New Hampshire, out of respect.” He is especially thankful for the role played by their part-time spiritual leader, “retired” Rabbi Emeritus Dov Pikelny of Marblehead who was invited to assist the congregation in 1981. He and his late wife Barbara were like angels. She was a wonderful cantress who reorganized our choir, and the rabbi has been just wonderful. He has given us things we can’t measure: a pride in Judaism, a love of learning and the chance to emulate our ancestors. We’re very thankful for him.”

Rabbi Pikelny, though not Sephardic himself, feels blessed to have been given the opportunity to work with the “wonderful people” who make up the congregation. Although he leads them during the High Holy days, the Congregation’s monthly Shabbat service is led by Gibely and the songs and prayers beautifully chanted by Cantress Joanne Pressman. Her husband Jerry is descended from Joseph Pernitchi, one of the original seven charter members. Gibely is also thankful for people like Thomas Bloom of Danvers who sounds the shofar on the High Holidays and Benjamin Bejar of Boston who reads from the Torah.

Sheryl Becker of Danvers, a warm and engaging Sephardic woman, and the youngest member of the Congregation, makes up in voice and spirit what the congregation lacks in number. Becker, 33, studied and became a bat mitzvah under Rabbi Pikelny over 20 years ago at Temple Shalom in Salem. Four years ago, she was involved in a car accident caused by a woman driving the wrong way on an exit ramp off Route 128. Her seven-month recovery required that she practically relearn everything she ever knew, including her name, how to walk, speak and read both English and Hebrew. Becker, who shortly after her recovery began attending services on a regular basis, exemplifies a love for and commitment to Tifereth Israel.

“I would go to synagogue and remember parts of some of the songs,” she said. “Then by singing them over and over, I learned how to read the Hebrew again. I never thought I’d be able to do this. It’s almost like being re-bat mitzvahed.”

Everyone was really supportive. They would ask me how I feel, and I could only raise my arms and say ‘I’m just happy to be.’ As the person in charge of calling everyone to come to services, she worries about the future of the congregation. “If I wasn’t here who knows if people would come to services or if the synagogue will go on. It bothers me. But I know I survived for a reason.”

Whether or not Tifereth Israel continues will depend largely on the commitment of the congregants to sustain the core membership, and increase the size of the congregation through outreach and programming. But as Gibely says, “The size of a congregation doesn’t matter. In Israel there’s a temple on every corner. Some have 15 members, some have 50. The important thing is to keep the continuity and tradition going. I believe Tifereth Israel will continue for a long time because we have a young nucleus of people, an active group of members, and a terrific Ladies Auxiliary.”

Tifereth Israel, 8 Pierpont Street in Peabody, conducts monthly services and welcomes the community to worship with them. For more information, contact the temple at 978-531-8135.
