Peabody home to both Ashkenazi, Sephardic traditions

By Judith S. Antonelli · Advocate Staff · Our Towns: Peabody

View original newspaper scan →

Original source: The Jewish Advocate, April 14–20, 1995, page 12. Archival scan I-581-01-15-035.

April 14–20, 1995

By Judith S. Antonelli · Advocate Staff

This week we continue our weekly series, Our Towns, that reports on the pulse of Jewish communities throughout Massachusetts. We explore Jewish life within that community, talk to synagogue leaders and residents, and provide readers with a close-up view of each town in the Commonwealth. This week, Our Towns visits Peabody.

Peabody’s Jewish history dates back to the turn of the century, when both Ashkenazi and Sephardic immigrants chose the thriving leather-manufacturing town as their new home.

By the 1930s, Peabody had 350 Jewish families, and by the mid-1960s, 1,700 Jewish families lived there. Today there is little movement into Peabody, but the population has remained stable. The current president of the city council is a Jewish woman.

Peabody is a major focal point of Jewish activity on the North Shore. It has a kosher butcher and bakery, and is the home of the Holocaust Center of the North Shore, directed by Sonia Weitz and Harriet Wacks.

Peabody also has its own Jewish Community Center, which split off from the JCC in Marblehead and became independent last September. The Peabody center serves 250 families in 22 communities with a day care center and a variety of programs for all ages. The JCC currently rents a building, but its long-range plans are to build a new center—perhaps on the grounds of Temple Ner Tamid, Peabody’s Conservative synagogue, says JCC President Debbie Poin.

Peabody’s oldest synagogue, Congregation Sons of Israel, was founded in 1909 by six families from Russia, Poland, and Lithuania. Another synagogue, Anshe Sfard, also came out of that original group, but it closed in the 1970s; its ark, memorial tablets, and remaining members became part of Congregation Sons of Israel.

Sons of Israel was Orthodox until 1962, when it modified its services with some English and instituted mixed seating. It now has 140 members, mostly elderly. By choice, the synagogue has no rabbi.

“We have a family spirit and are very close-knit,” said M. Irving Herbstner, ritual director. “We give kavod [honor] to everybody.”

A family service is held one Friday night a month. Adult education focuses on reading Hebrew and studying the prayers. Sons of Israel also serves as the headquarters of the Jewish War Veterans Post No. 220.

Around the same time that the Ashkenazi immigrants were starting Sons of Israel, a group of Sephardic Jews came to the area from Turkey. Settling first in the Castle Hill section of Salem, they moved to Peabody and formed Congregation Tifereth Israel in 1922, acquiring their own building in 1954.

Tifereth Israel, one of the oldest Sephardic congregations in New England, has 50 families. Its activities include holiday and anniversary celebrations, and meldathot—the Sephardic term for yahrzeit.

“We have a deep and proud tradition that existed prior to the Inquisition in Spain,” says President Joseph Gibely II. “We would never submit to Catholicism, so we went to Turkey. The Ottoman Empire was very tolerant. We got along with everyone and it was not unusual [for someone] to be able to speak four or five languages.”

“Our women picked up the cooking habits of all these groups,” he added, describing such delicacies as stuffed grape leaves, lamb with eggplant or string beans, stuffed tomatoes, and spinach pie.

As a youngster in Peabody, Gibely recalls, there was a great deal of discrimination by Ashkenazi Jews. A shopkeeper once referred to the Gibely family as “Gypsy Jews,” and another told him that Sephardic customs—such as naming a child after a living relative—were “wrong” and “not Jewish.”

“With maturity and evolution, that has changed,” Gibely declared. “There is enough trouble in this world without Jews fighting among themselves.” Tifereth Israel’s membership is open to Ashkenazi Jews (who adopt Sephardic prayer and pronunciation), the children are sent to Ashkenazi Hebrew schools, and the rabbi himself is Ashkenazi.

“A Jew is a Jew,” says Rabbi Dov Pikelny, who joined Tifereth Israel as a member after retiring from the rabbinate in 1980. “I chose to belong to this synagogue because it was peaceful, friendly, and had a good reputation. I thought I could serve them as an active member, but they asked me to be the rabbi. I agreed because it is such a wonderful group of people.”

In 1960 both a Conservative synagogue, Temple Ner Tamid, and a Reform synagogue, Temple Beth Shalom, were founded. Ner Tamid has 400 members and 150 students in its Hebrew school. Beth Shalom has 350 members and 240 students in its school.

“An interest in youth is what brought me into the rabbinate,” says Rabbi Abraham Morhaim, Ner Tamid’s rabbi for 29 years. As a result, the temple has three basketball teams and two volleyball teams and has won awards for its United Synagogue Youth programs.

Morhaim gave up his summer vacation 12 years in a row to take 65 teenagers to Israel for six weeks. Two other years he took them on a bus tour from New York to California, visiting Jewish communities and American historical sites.

“Teens are a very exciting group,” says Morhaim, who has been to Israel 35 times in 22 years. “They are energetic, bright, intelligent. If you learn how to reach them they can move mountains.”

Morhaim is retiring this year, but will continue in his other roles as instructor at the Gerim Institute (Conservative) and as chaplain of Children’s Hospital and the VA Hospital in Boston. He hopes to spend two or three months a year in Jerusalem.

Temple Beth Shalom’s specialty, according to Rabbi Philip Aronson, is providing “a meaningful liberal interpretation of Judaism for the North Shore... We have a warm and welcoming attitude towards intermarried families. The non-Jewish spouse is a member by marriage, and their backgrounds enrich us quite a bit.” Children raised as Jews are accepted as Jewish regardless of which parent is Jewish.

“Peabody has the largest concentration of unaffiliated Jews on the North Shore,” Aronson added. “We suspect a number of them may be intermarried, and we open our doors to them.”

Another unique program of Beth Shalom is the Chai Club, a “very lively group” of people who have been temple members for at least 18 years. “A number of them are founding members,” said Aronson, noting that the temple has a number of three-generation families as members.
