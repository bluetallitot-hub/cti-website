Origins of Peabody’s Sephardic Congregation Tifereth Israel

Their ancestors ran the helm when Columbus sailed. As the long-time president of Congregation Tifereth Israel (Sephardic) in Peabody, whose congregation comes from the entire North Shore, we recall that it was 500 years ago that the Jewish people were ordered out of the country if they would not embrace Catholicism.

Innocent citizens, who were Jewish, were arrested, tried and condemned to death. Early in 1481 the first auto da fé took place in Seville, when six men and women were burned alive. Shortly thereafter a permanent inquisition was constructed just outside the city walls for the executions by burning. During this period close to 50,000 Jews were murdered and more than 200,000 others were forced to convert to Catholicism.

The promise of free pardon for full confession encouraged widespread denunciation. Jews, particularly former synagogue officials, were enlisted at pain of excommunication to expose all they knew. The horrors of the Spanish Inquisition really got nasty in 1464 at the Concordat of Medina Del Campo in Spain, when it was decided to establish an inquiry into the conduct of the new Christians (conversos or Jews turned Catholics) and Marranos (Jews who practiced Christianity outwardly but secretly held on fiercely to the faith of their fathers, or that of which they were able to remember).

This determination to retain their Crypto-Jewish existence without books, without schools, without leadership and above all in constant danger of being discovered all added up to a very tragic time in our history.

The descendants and children of Sephardic immigrants from Spain are found throughout the Mediterranean world. Congregation Tifereth Israel in Peabody grew from that Sephardic tradition. Its members maintained customs, melodies and community practices that distinguished it from neighboring Ashkenazi congregations.

Congregation Tifereth Israel meets regularly at its synagogue at 8 Pierpont Street in Peabody. Rabbi Dov Pikelny is the spiritual leader. The congregation’s Sephardic synagogue is not just for Sephardic Jews; some members are Ashkenazi, and others come from mixed backgrounds.
