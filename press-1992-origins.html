1992 · Sephardic History

Commemorating 500 Years Since the Edict of Expulsion

Original headline: “IN SEARCH OF A HOME — Small synagogue remembers a big part of history”

The Salem Evening News · October 31, 1992 · A News staff report

Transcription

PEABODY — For Congregation Tifereth Israel, a small synagogue on Pierpont Street, the year 1492 means much more than the time Columbus set sail from Spain on his historic journey.

It is the year that Jews were driven from that country at the height of the Spanish Inquisition to begin a search for a home that centuries later led some of their descendants to the North Shore area. For that reason, the Pierpont Street congregation (made up of Sephardic-origin) Jews, remember King Ferdinand and Queen Isabella for a very different reason than the help they gave to Columbus.

To commemorate the Quincentennial Anniversary of the total expulsion of the Jews from Spain, the North Shore Jewish Historical Society and Congregation Sons of Israel will sponsor a combined forum at 8 p.m. Wednesday, Nov. 4.

Rabbi Abraham Morhaim of Temple Ner Tamid, a Sephardic Jew, will present an overview of the History of the Sephardim and Joseph Gibely, president of the synagogue, will trace the Sephardic history of the congregation.

Gibely takes great pride in his heritage.

“We still speak Ladino - a combination of Turkish, Greek, Spanish and English within our services,” he said noting that the first Sephardic families arrived on the North Shore from Constantinople (Istanbul) in 1911.

“The first to come were Leon Eskenas and Myer Benatar and their wives, Rebecca and Regina and, in quick order, they were followed by the following families: Gibely, Eskenazi, Yonis, Hasday, Havian, Leon, Behar, Pernitchi, Savy, Fermon and Erera,” he said. “When they first came they settled in the Castle Hill Section of Salem. They were small in number and couldn’t afford their own house of worship. But they were very devout Orthodox type Jews and continued to hold Friday night services in each others homes when they had enough men for a minyan (10 men are needed for an Orthodox service.) They carried the Sefer Torah from home to home on a rotational basis.”

Gibely said that coming as they did from Istanbul, the families all spoke four or five languages.

“But English wasn’t one of them and how they ever survived is beyond me but a lot of them went into business, bought cars and went to night school to learn English,” he said. “I’m pretty proud that my mother, Sarah Gibely, was the first woman of the Sephardic community to become an American citizen. She got the others to go to night school and 99 percent became citizens through the night school process.”

According to Gibely, the congregation is composed of Jews whose ancestors spent approximately 450 years in Constantinople after being forced out of Spain during the Inquisition. Many went to Turkey and Greece where they attained positions of importance. Though he can’t say for sure he feels it is highly possible that many congregants of today are descendants of these famous and highly respected Jews of yesterday.

“The names of many of our congregants today reflect this fact - Hasdei Iben Shaprut lived from 915 to 990. He was the vizier under Caliph Abd-Al-Rahman II - the name of the first president of this congregation was Isaac Hasday. In Christian Spain, the Apostate Caulos de Santa Maria was originally Solomon Halivi who lived from 1351 to 1435. He became Privy Councillor to King Henry III of Castille. A long time member of our congregation, now deceased, had practically the same name - Solomon Levy,” he said.

“One minister in Greece was called M. Bessantchi - Bessantchi was my mother’s maiden name. In the late 1800’s, Behor Ashenazi Effendi was the deputy in Turkey and one of our present members is Joshua Eskenazi. Another deputy, elected in 1908, was Emanuel Carasso and my brother-in-law had the very same name.”

Gibely also points with pride to the many “firsts” noted on these shores among the Sephardic Jews.

• The first soldier to serve in the armed forces of North American Lands was Hernando Alonso who served in the early 1500s.

• The first artist and silversmith in this country was Myer Myers born in New York in 1723.

• The first inter-colonial furrier was Hyman Levy born in 1760.

• The first merchant prince of the United States was Aaron Lopes who owned a fleet of 30 Trans Atlantic ships and over 100 coastwise vessels.

• The first American born clergyman, the Rev. Gershon Mendez Seixas, was born in 1745 and became rabbi of Congregation Sheareth Israel in New York in 1768.

• The first physician was Dr. Jacob Lumbrozo who arrived in Maryland on Jan. 24, 1656.

• The first to give his live for this country was Francis Salvador who was killed in 1776 at the Keowee River in South Carolina. Two years earlier, Salvador was elected to the general assembly of South Carolina - the first Jew in American History to serve in an elected office.

• The first Jewish Major in the armed forces was Benjamin Nones of the Continental Army. In 1780 Nones commanded a battalion of 400 men called the Hebrew Legion - Gibely says that it was so-named either because of its commander or because of its large number of Jewish enlistees.

• The first to found a settlement in the United States was Aaron Levy who founded Aaronsberg, Pennsylvania on Oct. 5, 1786. But of all the famous Sephardic firsts in which Gibely takes pride is the accomplishment of his very own cousin, Morris Gibeley.

“The city of Salem, Mass. was incorporated in 1626 and the first Jewish police officer in its long history is my cousin, Morris, who retired after 35 years on the force and I am proud to say today he is alive and well and a member of Congregation Tifereth Israel.”

Refreshments of Sephardic delicacies, prepared by the Ladies Auxiliary of Congregation Tifereth Israel will be served following the forum. Prior to the forum, at 7:30 p.m., the North Shore Jewish Historical Society will hold a brief installation conducted by Attorney Irving Herbster. They will then adjourn to the forum program upstairs at 8 p.m.
