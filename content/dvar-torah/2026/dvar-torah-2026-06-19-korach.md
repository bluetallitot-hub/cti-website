There is something remarkable — and perhaps a little pointed — about meeting a new community for the first time on Shabbat Korach.

If there is one parashah in the entire Torah about how not to lead, this is it.

Korach bursts onto the scene challenging authority, gathering followers, and stoking factionalism and insurrection — the Torah's first contested election, if you will, with no ballots, no campaign speeches, and admittedly far higher stakes than hanging chads.

For those who may not recall the details: Korach instigates a full-blown mutiny against Moshe and Aharon, demanding an equal share of power. The uprising ends, famously, with the earth opening and swallowing Korach and his followers alive.

So, if I am being introduced to you through Korach — let it serve as a cautionary tale.

## Korach'S Claim

Korach confronts Moshe and Aharon with a claim that is, on its face, entirely compelling:

כִּי כָל־הָעֵדָה כֻּלָּם קְדֹשִׁים

"For the entire congregation is holy."

And in many ways — he was right. Every person in a community matters. Every individual carries something intrinsically קָדוֹשׁ — sacred.

But Korach's failure lies in taking a profound spiritual truth and weaponizing it in the service of his ego. Instead of asking "How can I serve?" — he asks "Why not me?"

## The Lesson For Leadership

That shift — from "Why not me?" to "How can I serve?" — is at the very heart of what it means to be a rabbi.

And it is the primary question on my mind as we explore what it might look like to partner together.

When challenged, Moshe's very first response is

וַיִּפֹּל עַל־פָּנָיו — he falls on his face.

He pauses. He listens. He literally and physically grounds himself before uttering a single word.

That is precisely how meaningful rabbinic presence ought to begin.

The calling of a rabbi is to lift up the prayers of the people already in the room — to look out at a congregation and genuinely seek to learn:

What are the shared hopes of this place?
What comfort or inspiration do you need?
Which melodies and traditions are most cherished?
And ultimately — How can I serve as the vessel for your prayers?

## Closing

Because Korach — despite everything — was right about one fundamental truth: this community is already holy. It does not need a visiting rabbi or any rabbi to create holiness.

The real opportunity is to step up to the Taiva and unlock the holiness that already lives within every soul in this sanctuary — to ensure that your connection to Torah, to one another, and to

El Dio, El Kadosh Baruj Hú

feels vibrant, accessible, and alive.

Leadership is not about standing above the community. It is about standing among it.

The holiest, most enduring spaces are built not on ego, but on trust and authentic relationship.

If we find that we are a good fit for this coming High Holiday season, those are exactly the kinds of connections — trusting, meaningful, and lasting — that I would hope to build with each of you.

And thankfully, unlike Korach's assembly, if this doesn't go perfectly tonight — we can at least trust that the ground will stay exactly where it is.

Shabat de paz i bien
