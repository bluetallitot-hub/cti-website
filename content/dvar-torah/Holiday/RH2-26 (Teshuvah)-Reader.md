תשובה — teshuvah.

Reverend Dr. Desola Pool translates it as “repentance.” But my third-grade Hebrew teacher, Shifra, taught me that when you really want to understand a Hebrew word, you look for its shoresh — its root. So once again, I went back to my roots.

ש־ו־ב — shuv Shin vav bet.

To turn. To return. To come back. But before we get to return, there is another meaning of teshuvah that I think comes first. If someone asks you a question in Hebrew, you can say:

מה התשובה? — Mah ha-teshuvah?

What is the teshuvah — what is the answer? The same word that Reverend Dr. Desola Pool translates here as “repentance” is also the ordinary Hebrew word for an answer. And Judaism has an entire body of literature called:

שאלות ותשובות — She’elot U-Teshuvot.

Questions and teshuvot. Questions and answers. Responsa. For centuries, Jews have brought real-life questions to rabbis, and the responses — the teshuvot — applied Jewish law to situations people were actually facing. In other words, it was a little like Dear Abby, except with more footnotes and considerably more Aramaic. And the tradition continues. Today, of course, many people skip the rabbi entirely and just ask Rabbi Google. And those questions were not always abstract: What should I do when my child is absolutely certain they are right — and they are totally wrong?

What should I do when Jewish law says one thing, modern life says another, and my mother has a third opinion? What should I do when I know exactly what everyone else ought to do, but somehow the halakhah becomes much less clear when the question is about me? And the answers — the responses — are called teshuvot. That should not surprise us. Judaism is a religion that makes room for questions. Faith does not require us to stop thinking. We argue. We challenge. We wrestle. After all, even the name Yisrael is bound up with the idea of wrestling with God. And, of course:

If you ask two Jews one question and you’ll get three teshuvot — plus one person explaining why you asked the wrong question. So Rosh Hashanah is not asking us to stop questioning. It is asking something harder: What will our teshuvah be once the question has been asked? Because not every question has a satisfying answer. There are things in our lives that we may never fully understand. But even when we cannot give a teshuvah in the sense of an answer, we can still give a teshuvah in the sense of a response. That is the distinction I want to make.

God asks us for a teshuvah — not necessarily the answer, but our response. We may not know why something happened. But our teshuvah can be what we decide to do next. We may not know exactly how to fix ourselves or our relationships. But our teshuvah can be to begin. And that is where teshuvah starts. Not with certainty. With a teshuvah — with a response.

## Teshuvah as Return

Where will that response take you? Because a real teshuvah does not stay in the realm of thought. It moves us. And that brings us back to the root:

שוב — shuv — return.

We already know this language from our prayers. When the Torah is returned, we say: Shuvah lim’onach (Return to Your dwelling place.) — in a couple of minutes, as we return the Torah to the ark. That image of return is important, but I think we sometimes misunderstand what “return” means. Teshuvah does not mean going backward in time. It does not mean becoming the person you were ten or twenty or forty years ago. It does not mean erasing everything that happened in between.

It is more like returning to a path. In modern Hebrew, a person who becomes religious is called a chozer b’teshuvah — literally, one who returns in teshuvah. And we actually say this same language in Avinu Malkeinu:

החזירנו בתשובה שלמה לפניך

— Hachazireinu b’teshuvah sheleimah lefanecha

“Return us before You in complete teshuvah.”

The language is very redundant in English — return us in return — but in Hebrew it makes the point beautifully. Teshuvah is not simply feeling differently. It is being brought back toward the path. If you are hiking and wander off the trail, you do not need to go all the way back to the exact place where you first stepped off. You need to find your way back onto the path from where you are now. That, I think, is teshuvah. Teshuvah is not about returning to where you were. It is about returning to where you are supposed to be heading. And we return as the people we are now. With our history. With our mistakes.

With our losses. With our scars. When you cut your arm, healing does not mean the skin becomes exactly what it was before. The wound closes. The body becomes healthy again. But a scar may remain. And that scar is not proof that healing failed. It is proof that healing happened. There’s a song by Train called “Bruises” that says, “Everybody loses… we all got bruises.” That feels right to me. We do not come back untouched. We come back carrying the bruises and scars of having lived. Scars show that you have lived. I think spiritual life can work the same way. Teshuvah does not erase the past.

It does not erase the wrong choices we made or the wounds we carry. It means returning to a healthier spiritual state with the life we have actually lived. A healthier relationship with ourselves. A healthier relationship with a partner or someone we love. A healthier relationship with God. Not perfection. Health. Not going backward. Getting back on the path. God expects teshuvah, not perfection.

## Teshuvah and Restoration

From the same shin vav bet ש־ו־ב family comes another word:

השבה — hashavah.

Returning something.

Restoration.

We know it from השבת אבידה — hashavat aveidah, the mitzvah of returning a lost object. In my house, this mitzvah is usually fulfilled when I lose my keys and my wife finds them. But the idea goes much deeper than lost objects. If I have harmed someone, recognizing it and regretting it matter. Apologizing matters. Changing my behavior matters. But if the harm left something broken, teshuvah also asks me to repair what I can. If I took something, I return it. If I caused a financial loss, I repay it. If I damaged trust, I begin the work of rebuilding it.

Repentance cannot be only about repairing my soul while leaving the damage in your life untouched. That is why: Teshuvah requires hashavah. Returning to the path also means taking responsibility for what happened while we were off it. Not everything can be repaired completely. Some words cannot be unsaid. Some time cannot be returned. Some wounds leave scars. But teshuvah still asks us to restore what we can.

Response. Return. Restoration.

It is a response. It is a return — getting back onto the path. And it is restoration — repairing what we can.

Response.
Return.
Restoration.

And all three require action. Yesterday, pakad meant showing up. Today, teshuvah is what we do once we have shown up. We respond. We return. We restore. And God does not expect perfection. God asks us for a teshuvah.

## The Limit of Teshuvah

I can recognize what I did. I can apologize. I can change. I can restore what can be restored. But I cannot make another person forgive me. That is no longer entirely in my hands. And that brings us toward Yom Kippur and another word:

מחילה — mechilah.

Teshuvah asks: What must I do now that I know something has to change? Yom Kippur asks the next question: What happens when repair becomes a question of forgiveness? For today, though, the question is ours. Life will ask things of us. God will ask things of us. We will not always have the answers. But we can still have a teshuvah. So this Rosh Hashanah, what will your teshuvah be — how will you respond, where do you need to return, and what do you need to restore?

Shanah tovah. Buen anyo. i Muchos anyos.
