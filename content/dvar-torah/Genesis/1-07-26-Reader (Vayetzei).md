Different Languages, Same Table
Parashat Vayetzei | Genesis 28:10–32:3


Thanksgiving is almost here. And for many families, that means gathering around a table with people who share our history, our memories, and sometimes even our childhood bedrooms.


And yet, by the time dessert arrives, we may discover that we seem to be speaking entirely different languages.


I'm not talking about the relative who insists on explaining cryptocurrency. Although that can certainly make for a long Thanksgiving dinner.


I'm talking about the fact that two people can share the same experiences and remember them completely differently. One remembers a wonderful family vacation. The other remembers twelve hours trapped in the back seat without air conditioning.


Neither necessarily remembers incorrectly. They simply experienced the same events from different seats.


This week's parasha, Vayetzei, offers a remarkably familiar family story.


Yaakov spends twenty years living and working with his uncle Lavan. During those years, he marries Lavan's daughters, raises a family, and accumulates considerable wealth. But their relationship is marked by deception, resentment, and competing claims about what each owes the other.


Lavan deceives Yaakov over his marriages. Yaakov complains that Lavan has repeatedly changed his wages. Eventually, Yaakov leaves without telling Lavan, taking his family and possessions with him.


Lavan pursues him.


When they finally confront one another, both men have grievances. And both seem convinced that the other has behaved unfairly.


Yaakov recounts his twenty years of labor, the hardships he endured, and the wages he believes Lavan manipulated. From Yaakov's perspective, he has earned everything he takes with him.


Lavan sees things differently. He declares:


הַבָּנוֹת בְּנֹתַי וְהַבָּנִים בָּנַי וְהַצֹּאן צֹאנִי


“The daughters are my daughters, the children are my children, and the flocks are my flocks.” (Genesis 31:43)


It is an extraordinary claim. After twenty years of Yaakov's labor, Lavan still considers nearly everything to belong to him.


But it also reveals something about the way Lavan understands the family. He sees his daughters, grandchildren, and the household Yaakov has built as extensions of his own.


Yaakov sees a family and livelihood he has struggled to establish independently.


They share history. They do not share the story.


And then the Torah gives us a remarkable little detail.


The two men gather stones and establish a covenant. Lavan names the mound יְגַר שָׂהֲדוּתָא (Yegar Sahaduta), in Aramaic. Yaakov calls it גַּלְעֵד (Gal'ed), in Hebrew (Genesis 31:47).


Both names mean essentially the same thing: a mound of witness.


After twenty years together, they cannot even agree on what language to use when naming a pile of rocks.


But perhaps the difference is not merely linguistic.


They are standing before the same stones, acknowledging the same agreement, and remembering the same twenty years. Yet each gives the mound a name in his own language.


Neither adopts the other's words.


And neither abandons his own understanding of what brought them there.


That is often how family disagreements work.


We assume that because we experienced something together, we must remember it the same way. When someone tells a different version, we may interpret that difference as dishonesty, ingratitude, or even betrayal.


Sometimes, of course, people really have behaved dishonestly. The Torah gives us little reason to idealize either Yaakov or Lavan.


But even when we are justified in our grievances, we do not necessarily understand the entire experience of the person standing across from us.


What happens next is particularly important.


Yaakov and Lavan establish a boundary. Most families disagree about where to spend the holidays. Yaakov and Lavan need an international border.


The mound will serve as a witness between them. Neither will cross toward the other לְרָעָה — “for harm” (Genesis 31:52).


Notice what they do not do.


They do not agree about the past. They do not apologize for every deception. The Torah does not tell us that either man changes his mind about the other's behavior.


And it certainly does not describe a warm reconciliation.


Instead, they establish a limit.


Whatever happened between them, neither will use it as justification to harm the other.


There is something deeply practical about this resolution.


We often imagine that repairing relationships requires complete agreement: everyone must acknowledge the same facts, accept the same blame, and arrive at the same interpretation of what happened.


But sometimes that agreement is impossible.


And sometimes preserving a relationship—or at least preventing further harm—requires something more modest.


We can disagree without being destructive.


We can establish boundaries without seeking revenge.


We can recognize that another person has a different understanding of our shared past without surrendering our own.


And then comes one of my favorite details in the entire episode.


After the covenant, Yaakov offers a sacrifice and invites his kinsmen לֶאֱכָל־לָחֶם — “to eat bread,” or, as we commonly say, to break bread (Genesis 31:54).


After twenty years of disagreements, the story ends with a meal.


The Torah does not tell us that everyone suddenly agrees about what happened. The mound is still standing. The boundary remains in place.


But the final image is not of an argument.


It is of people eating together.


As Thanksgiving approaches, perhaps that is a lesson worth carrying to our own tables.


Our families are full of shared experiences and competing memories. We may disagree about decisions made years ago, about what someone meant by a particular remark, or about matters much larger than either.


Some disagreements are trivial. Others are serious, and we should not pretend otherwise.


But the person sitting across the table is more than the disagreement we have with them.


Being right about something doesn't give us the right to be cruel about it.


Yaakov and Lavan did not need to speak the same language. They needed to recognize a boundary neither should cross.


And perhaps that is what makes sharing a table possible.


Not that we have forgotten every grievance. Not that we agree about everything. And certainly not that we have abandoned our convictions.


But that we have chosen not to let our disagreements become the entirety of our relationships.


We do not have to share the same story to share the same table.


And perhaps, this Thanksgiving, so can we.
