There is a wonderful bit of Hebrew wordplay at the beginning of the Torah that is easy to miss in English.

At the end of Genesis chapter 2, Adam and Eve are described as being naked and unashamed.

Then, in the very next verse, we meet the serpent, who is described as cunning.

In Hebrew, the words for naked and cunning sound remarkably similar.

That is not an accident of translation. The Torah places these words side by side, almost inviting us to notice the contrast.

Adam and Eve are naked, innocent, and completely unaware of their vulnerability. The serpent, on the other hand, understands something they do not. He knows how to manipulate, how to persuade, and how to use someone else's innocence to his advantage.

It is a contrast that every parent eventually comes to understand.

When our children are young, we try to protect them from the world. We shield them from things they are not ready to understand. We try to preserve their innocence for as long as we can.

And then, inevitably, they begin to discover that the world is more complicated than we have allowed them to believe.

People are not always truthful. Appearances can be misleading. Not everyone has their best interests at heart.

And perhaps most unsettling of all, they discover that they themselves can conceal things, too.

That is what makes the next part of the Garden of Eden story so fascinating.

After Adam and Eve eat from the forbidden tree, the Torah tells us:

> 

“Then the eyes of both of them were opened.” — Genesis 3:7

What exactly has changed?

They have not suddenly become evil. But they have become more complicated.

They recognize their nakedness. They make coverings for themselves. They hide from God. And when God confronts them, they begin explaining why someone else is responsible.

In one afternoon, humanity discovers embarrassment, concealment, and the art of shifting blame.

Quite an educational experience for a single piece of fruit.

But beneath the humor is something profoundly human.

Adam and Eve have acquired knowledge they cannot unlearn. They now understand vulnerability, both their own and that of others. They can use that understanding to protect themselves, but they can also use it to deceive.

**Their disobedience was the mistake. Their changed awareness is the lasting consequence.**

And that brings us to what I find perhaps the most moving moment in the entire story.

After God confronts Adam and Eve and announces the consequences of their actions, the Torah tells us:

> 

“And the Lord God made garments of skin for Adam and his wife, and clothed them.” — Genesis 3:21

Think about that for a moment.

Adam and Eve have disobeyed God. They have attempted to hide what they have done. They have even tried to shift responsibility.

And what does God do?

**He makes them clothing.**

They had already tried to cover themselves with fig leaves. God provides something more substantial.

It is an extraordinary parental gesture.

God does not excuse their behavior. He does not remove the consequences. But neither does He abandon them.

He prepares them for what comes next.

And what comes next is difficult.

Adam and Eve must leave Eden.

The Torah explains that God prevents them from reaching the Tree of Life and living forever. But their departure also creates an image that I think every parent can recognize.

**They cannot go back to being the children they were before.**

Their innocence is gone. Their understanding of the world has changed. And even God, in this reading, must accept that the relationship has changed with it.

We sometimes talk about parenting as knowing when to let go.

But I think there is something even harder involved.

**Acceptance.**

Accepting that our children will encounter things we would rather they did not encounter.

Accepting that they will discover painful truths about the world.

Accepting that they will make mistakes, sometimes the very mistakes we warned them against.

And accepting that we cannot protect them from every disappointment, every bad decision, or every person who might take advantage of them.

We can teach them. We can guide them. We can establish boundaries and insist on consequences when necessary.

**But we cannot keep them in Eden forever.**

And perhaps we shouldn't want to.

Because the goal of raising children is not to preserve their innocence indefinitely. It is to help them become capable, responsible adults who can navigate a world that is not always innocent.

There is one final detail in the Torah that keeps this from becoming a terribly sad story.

**Bereishit does not end with the expulsion from Eden.**

The story continues.

Adam and Eve begin their lives outside the Garden. They become parents themselves. Human life continues, with all its difficulties, possibilities, and responsibilities.

They cannot return to Eden.

But they have a future.

And God has not abandoned them.

That, I think, is the great hope embedded in this story.

Our children will grow up. They will lose some of their innocence. They will discover things we wish they never had to discover.

But they will also learn, mature, develop judgment, and become people capable of making their own way in the world.

And if we have done our job well, they will carry something of what we taught them into that world.

**The loss of innocence may be the price of independence.**

**But the end of innocence is not the end of hope.**

Buen Shabbat. Shabbat Shalom.
