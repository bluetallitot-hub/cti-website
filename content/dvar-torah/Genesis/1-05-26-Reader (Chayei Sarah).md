# Faith Without Conditions


*Parashat Chayei Sarah*


Have you ever tried to make a deal with God?


You know the kind.


“God, if You just help me get through this one situation, I promise I'll be a better person.”


Or perhaps something more specific.


“God, if this is really what You want me to do, give me a sign.”


And not just any sign. Something unmistakable.


Of course, the trouble with asking God for a sign is that we're not always satisfied with the first one.


“God, if You want me to do this, make the light turn green.”


The light turns green.


“Well, that could have been a coincidence.”


Before you know it, you've driven halfway to New Hampshire and you're still waiting for divine confirmation.


This week's parasha, Chayei Sarah, tells the story of Abraham sending his trusted servant to find a wife for Isaac.


It's an enormously important mission. The future of Abraham's family, and the promises God has made to him, depend upon what happens next.


Abraham gives his servant specific instructions. The woman must come from Abraham's homeland and family. Isaac must remain in Canaan. And the woman must be willing to come.


Abraham also assures his servant that God will guide the journey:


**הוּא יִשְׁלַח מַלְאָכוֹ לְפָנֶיךָ**


“He will send His angel before you.” (Genesis 24:7)


Personally, I would think that was a fairly impressive guarantee.


But when the servant reaches his destination, he asks God for a sign.


And that raises an interesting question.


**When does asking God for guidance become telling God how that guidance must appear?**


## Abraham's Faith, Eliezer's Responsibility


Before accepting his assignment, Abraham's servant, traditionally identified as Eliezer, asks a perfectly reasonable question:


**אוּלַי לֹא־תֹאבֶה הָאִשָּׁה לָלֶכֶת אַחֲרַי**


“Perhaps the woman will not be willing to follow me.” (Genesis 24:5)


In other words, “Abraham, I understand the assignment. But what happens if she says no?”


That's not a lack of faith. That's good judgment.


Abraham explains that if the woman refuses, Eliezer will be released from his oath. But under no circumstances may he take Isaac back to Abraham's homeland.


Notice what Abraham has done.


He's established the mission, defined the boundaries, and explained what happens if the desired outcome cannot be achieved.


Eliezer is responsible for carrying out the assignment faithfully. He is not responsible for forcing someone to accept it.


**Being responsible for our actions does not mean being responsible for controlling the outcome.**


Abraham understands that distinction.


He trusts that God will guide the mission, but he also recognizes that the woman has a choice.


Faith, for Abraham, doesn't eliminate uncertainty. It allows him to act responsibly despite uncertainty.


And so Eliezer sets out.


He travels to the city of Nahor, arriving at the well where the women come to draw water.


He's reached the right place. He's followed Abraham's instructions.


Now he needs to find the right woman.


And this is where things become interesting.


## When Faith Wants a Guarantee


Eliezer begins with a perfectly reasonable prayer:


**ה׳ אֱלֹהֵי אֲדֹנִי אַבְרָהָם הַקְרֵה־נָא לְפָנַי הַיּוֹם**


“O Lord, God of my master Abraham, grant me success today.” (Genesis 24:12)


There's nothing wrong with asking God for help.


But Eliezer goes further.


He proposes a test. When he asks a young woman for water, if she gives him a drink and then volunteers to water all ten camels, he will know that she is the woman God has chosen for Isaac.


Now, let's give Eliezer some credit.


He's looking for generosity, kindness, and a willingness to help someone without being asked.


Watering ten camels isn't a small favor. It's practically volunteering to fill a swimming pool with a small jug.


Eliezer has chosen a meaningful characteristic.


But here's where I think he makes a mistake.


**He takes a reasonable criterion for judging someone's character and turns it into a condition for recognizing God's will.**


Abraham told him to find a suitable woman. He assured him that God would guide the mission.


But Abraham never instructed him to devise a sign.


There's a difference between saying, “God, please guide me toward the right decision,” and saying, “God, I've worked out exactly how I'd like You to guide me.”


Eliezer clearly believes in God. He believes strongly enough to pray.


But he wants to establish the terms under which he'll recognize God's help.


And then something remarkable happens.


Before Eliezer has even finished praying, Rebecca arrives.


He asks her for water. She gives him a drink. And without being asked, she offers to water his camels.


Exactly as he had requested.


You might think that settles everything.


But the Torah tells us:


**וְהָאִישׁ מִשְׁתָּאֵה לָהּ מַחֲרִישׁ לָדַעַת הַהִצְלִיחַ ה׳ דַּרְכּוֹ אִם־לֹא**


“The man stood gazing at her in silence, wondering whether the Lord had made his journey successful or not.” (Genesis 24:21)


Think about that.


Eliezer asked for a specific sign. The sign occurred.


**And he still doesn't know whether his mission has succeeded!**


He must still investigate Rebecca's family, determine whether she is suitable, and discover whether she is willing to go.


The sign hasn't eliminated his responsibility.


It hasn't replaced his judgment.


And it certainly hasn't guaranteed the outcome.


Perhaps that's the irony of the entire episode. Eliezer wanted certainty, but even after receiving his sign, he still had to confront uncertainty.


## What the Rabbis Saw


The rabbis themselves debated Eliezer's behavior.


The Talmud (Chullin 95b) discusses *nichush*, divination, and specifically mentions Eliezer's sign as an example.


Maimonides takes the concern seriously, citing Eliezer when discussing the prohibition against establishing signs to determine one's actions.


But other authorities, including the Raavad and Tosafot, challenge or qualify that interpretation.


After all, Eliezer wasn't looking for an arbitrary omen. He was looking for a woman who demonstrated *chesed*, loving-kindness.


And Rebecca certainly demonstrated that.


The distinction matters.


There's nothing wrong with evaluating someone's character. That was part of Eliezer's responsibility.


But why did he need to turn that evaluation into a sign from God?


He could have recognized Rebecca's generosity without prescribing the circumstances in advance.


**We should trust God. But we still have to verify our own judgments.**


And Eliezer's sign, however successful, did not relieve him of that responsibility.


## Rebecca's Choice


Throughout this story, we've concentrated on Abraham and Eliezer.


Abraham gives the instructions. Eliezer travels, prays, establishes his sign, and negotiates with Rebecca's family.


But there's another person involved.


Rebecca.


And the Torah doesn't allow her simply to disappear into someone else's plans.


After hearing Eliezer's account, Rebecca's family declares:


**מֵה׳ יָצָא הַדָּבָר**


“The matter has come from the Lord.” (Genesis 24:50)


That sounds rather definitive.


Abraham trusts God's promise. Eliezer believes his prayer has been answered. Rebecca's family acknowledges divine providence.


You might think there's nothing left to discuss.


But when the question arises of whether Rebecca will leave immediately, her family calls her and asks:


**הֲתֵלְכִי עִם־הָאִישׁ הַזֶּה**


“Will you go with this man?” (Genesis 24:58)


And Rebecca answers:


**אֵלֵךְ**


“I will go.”


Think about the courage contained in those two Hebrew syllables.


Rebecca is leaving her home, her family, and everything familiar to travel to a distant land and marry a man she has never met.


Does that sound familiar?


Earlier in Genesis, God told Abraham:


**לֶךְ־לְךָ מֵאַרְצְךָ וּמִמּוֹלַדְתְּךָ וּמִבֵּית אָבִיךָ**


“Go forth from your land, from your birthplace, and from your father's house.” (Genesis 12:1)


The circumstances are different. Abraham received a direct command from God; Rebecca receives a marriage proposal through Abraham's servant.


But both are asked to leave everything familiar behind and travel toward a future they cannot yet see.


And both are willing to go.


Rebecca doesn't demand a sign. She doesn't insist upon a guarantee that her marriage will be happy or that life in Canaan will be easy.


She simply says:


**אֵלֵךְ — “I will go”**


Perhaps that's what makes Rebecca such an appropriate partner for Isaac.


Yes, she is generous. Yes, she demonstrates extraordinary kindness at the well.


But she also possesses something that connects her directly to Abraham.


**The courage to move forward without demanding certainty about the outcome.**


And there's one more detail worth noticing.


Rebecca's family has already declared that this marriage comes from God.


Yet Rebecca is still asked whether she will go.


The Torah places her choice alongside the family's recognition of divine providence.


It doesn't explain how God's plan and Rebecca's free will fit together. Philosophers have debated that question for centuries, and I don't expect to settle it before Kiddush.


But the Torah shows us that Rebecca's decision matters.


She isn't simply a passenger being carried along by a divine plan.


She chooses to participate.


Abraham trusts. Eliezer seeks reassurance. Rebecca chooses.


Three different responses to uncertainty.


## Trusting Without Conditions


There's nothing wrong with praying for an outcome.


We pray for healing, for success, for our families, and for guidance when we face difficult decisions.


And we should.


But there's a difference between asking God for help and telling God what the answer must look like.


There's a difference between praying for an outcome and making our faith depend upon receiving that outcome.


Abraham accepted that he could fulfill his responsibility without controlling the result.


Eliezer reminds us how difficult it can be to resist the desire for one more sign.


And Rebecca demonstrates the courage to choose a path without knowing exactly where it will lead.


Faith doesn't mean we stop asking questions. It doesn't mean abandoning judgment or responsibility.


Quite the opposite.


We must think, choose, act, and fulfill our obligations.


But when we've done everything within our power, we must recognize that the rest is not ours to command.


**Faith isn't a contract in which we agree to trust God only if God agrees to deliver the outcome we've requested.**


We can ask. We can hope. We can even plead.


But we cannot make our trust conditional upon getting the answer we want.


Perhaps the problem isn't that God hasn't sent us enough signs.


Perhaps it's that we're waiting for certainty when what we really need is the courage to act.


To do what is right. To take responsibility for the choices before us. And to trust that we don't need to know the entire journey before taking the next step.


Because faith isn't telling God how the road must unfold.


**Faith is doing our part, trusting God with what lies beyond our control, and having the courage to say, like Rebecca:**


**אֵלֵךְ — “I will go”**
