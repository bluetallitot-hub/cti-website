# Rebecca: The CEO of Abraham's Family

*D'var Torah · Parashat Toledot · November 2026*  
*A Reader's Edition · Rabbi Phillip Blue*

---

## The Most Expensive Lunch in History

Esau may have eaten the most expensive lunch in history.

He returns from the field, exhausted and hungry, and finds his brother Jacob preparing lentil stew. He asks for some of that red stuff, and Jacob offers him a deal: the stew in exchange for his birthright.

Now, I've had some expensive lunches in my life, but I've never paid for one with my children's inheritance.

We can certainly question Jacob's behavior. Taking advantage of your brother when he is hungry is hardly an example of brotherly love. And if we are looking for a biblical role model for ethical business practices, we might want to keep looking.

But the Torah's judgment at the end of this episode is directed not at Jacob, but at Esau.

**וַיִּבֶז עֵשָׂו אֶת־הַבְּכֹרָה**

*“Esau despised the birthright.”* — Genesis 25:34

The problem is not simply that Esau made a bad deal. The problem is that he failed to appreciate what he possessed.

In Abraham's family, the question of inheritance carried implications far beyond wealth. God had established a covenant with Abraham, and someone would have to assume responsibility for carrying that covenant forward. Yet when confronted with a bowl of lentils, Esau shows little interest in what his birthright might mean for his future.

What makes this particularly interesting is that there is someone else in the family whose actions stand in almost complete contrast to Esau's.

His mother, Rebecca.

## Isaac the Manager, Rebecca the CEO

Last week, in Chayei Sarah, we watched Abraham go to extraordinary lengths to find a wife for Isaac. He sends his most trusted servant on a journey to his homeland, makes him swear an oath, and insists that Isaac himself not leave the land of Canaan.

We tend to read this as the story of a father trying to find a suitable wife for his son. But perhaps Abraham was doing something more. Perhaps he understood that the future of his family would depend as much on the woman Isaac married as on Isaac himself.

And perhaps Abraham understood something about his son.

Isaac is a remarkably capable man. In this week's parasha, he prospers during a famine, reaping a hundredfold from the crops he plants. He manages a substantial household, navigates disputes over wells, and ultimately negotiates a treaty with Avimelech. He has inherited his father's enterprise and is quite successful at running it.

But if Abraham's family were a business, I might describe Isaac as an excellent manager.

**Rebecca, on the other hand, is the CEO.**

A manager makes sure the operation runs well. A CEO has the additional responsibility of determining where the operation is going and who will eventually lead it.

Perhaps Abraham's search for a wife for Isaac was, in part, a search for someone who could help answer those questions.

Consider the woman he found. Rebecca encounters a stranger at a well and volunteers to water his camels, a considerable undertaking that nobody has asked of her. Later, when her family asks whether she is willing to leave everything familiar behind and travel with this stranger, she answers with a single word:

**אֵלֵךְ — “I will go”**

Last week, we saw these actions as expressions of Rebecca's character and faith. This week, I think we begin to understand why those qualities were so important to the future of Abraham's family.

Rebecca is a woman who takes initiative. She is willing to make difficult decisions, and she is not afraid of the uncertainty that comes with them.

## Rebecca Sees the Future

When Rebecca experiences difficulty during her pregnancy, she goes to inquire of the Lord. The answer she receives is extraordinary. Two nations are in her womb, and one will be stronger than the other.

**וְרַב יַעֲבֹד צָעִיר**

*“The elder shall serve the younger.”* — Genesis 25:23

It is worth comparing this prophecy with the revelations Isaac receives in our parasha. During the famine, God tells Isaac not to go down to Egypt and renews the covenant He made with Abraham. Later, at Beersheba, God appears to Isaac again, promising to bless him and multiply his descendants.

The difference between Isaac and Rebecca is not that one has a relationship with God and the other does not. It is that the revelations they receive concern different aspects of the family's destiny.

Isaac receives confirmation of the covenant he has inherited. Rebecca receives a prophecy concerning the relationship between his two heirs.

The Torah never tells us whether Rebecca shares her prophecy with Isaac.

We are told that Isaac loves Esau because of the game he brings him, while Rebecca loves Jacob. It would be easy to read this as a story of two parents playing favorites. But Rebecca has been given a reason to question the expectation that the older son will succeed his father, and as her sons grow older, their behavior may well influence how she understands that prophecy.

Esau's attitude toward his birthright gives her good reason for concern. Then, at the end of chapter 26, he marries two Hittite women.

The Torah tells us that they were *“a source of bitterness to Isaac and Rebecca.”* — Genesis 26:35

Notice that Isaac shares Rebecca's concern. Both parents recognize a problem, yet in the very next chapter, Isaac prepares to bless Esau.

I think Rebecca understands something about the importance of Esau's marriages that goes beyond the immediate difficulties they cause within the household.

The responsibility for the future of a family does not belong to the husband alone. His wife has an equally important role in determining what that family will become. If Esau is to carry Abraham's covenant forward, his choice of wife matters enormously.

Abraham understood this when he sent his servant to find a wife for Isaac. Rebecca understands it because she has spent her adult life fulfilling that very responsibility.

She knows how important the next matriarch will be because she herself is the matriarch Abraham chose.

## The Succession Plan

When Isaac grows old and his eyesight fails, he calls Esau and asks him to hunt game and prepare a meal. Afterward, Isaac intends to bless him.

Rebecca overhears the conversation and takes matters into her own hands. She prepares the food Isaac loves, dresses Jacob in Esau's clothing, and covers his hands and neck with goatskins.

Jacob hesitates, but his objection is revealing. He does not ask whether it is right to deceive his father. He worries that his father will discover the deception and curse him instead of blessing him.

Rebecca answers:

**עָלַי קִלְלָתְךָ בְּנִי**

*“Let your curse be upon me, my son.”* — Genesis 27:13

Rebecca is willing to assume responsibility for her decision.

Of course, we cannot discuss her leadership without acknowledging what she is doing. She is deceiving her husband and asking her son to participate. Even if she is right about Jacob, does that justify her actions?

The Torah rarely gives us leaders whose decisions are beyond criticism. Rebecca is no exception, and I don't think we need to defend everything she does in order to recognize her leadership.

What interests me even more than the deception itself is what Rebecca does afterward.

Esau, furious at what has happened, resolves to kill Jacob after Isaac's death. Rebecca learns of his intentions and tells Jacob to flee to her brother Laban until Esau's anger subsides.

But when Rebecca speaks to Isaac, she does not mention Esau's threat. Instead, she returns to a concern that she and Isaac already share.

*“I am disgusted with my life because of the daughters of Heth.”* — Genesis 27:46

If Jacob also marries a Hittite woman, she asks, what good is her life to her?

Perhaps Rebecca is simply using an argument that she knows will persuade Isaac. But I'm not convinced that it is merely a pretext. If Rebecca believes that Jacob is destined to carry Abraham's covenant forward, then finding the right wife for him is every bit as important as finding the right wife for Isaac was a generation earlier.

Isaac responds by sending Jacob to Paddan-Aram, to Rebecca's family, with instructions to find a wife there.

Before Jacob leaves, however, Isaac does something that I think is central to understanding this entire story.

He blesses him again.

This time, there is no deception. Isaac knows exactly whom he is blessing.

**וְיִתֶּן־לְךָ אֶת־בִּרְכַּת אַבְרָהָם**

*“May He grant you the blessing of Abraham.”* — Genesis 28:4

The blessing that Jacob received through deception in chapter 27 promised prosperity and authority. Here, Isaac explicitly invokes the blessing of Abraham, including the inheritance of the land promised to his family.

Did Isaac ever intend to give Esau the blessing of Abraham, or did he understand the two blessings differently?

Whatever the answer, Rebecca's intervention has led to a remarkable outcome. Jacob is no longer simply fleeing from his brother. He is leaving with his father's permission to find a wife, and he carries with him the covenantal blessing that Isaac has given him knowingly and willingly.

I wonder how much of this Rebecca had anticipated. Was the deception merely the first step in a larger strategy? Perhaps she was trying, all along, to get Jacob to the right place to find the right wife and establish the next generation of Abraham's family.

Or perhaps she was responding to each new crisis as it arose.

The Torah leaves that question open. But Rebecca's understanding of how to persuade Isaac is remarkable. She has found a way to protect Jacob while addressing the very concern that she and her husband share about the future of their family.

And Isaac's final blessing reminds us that Rebecca's leadership does not come at the expense of his own. Whatever disagreements they may have had about their children, Isaac ultimately recognizes Jacob as the heir to Abraham's covenant.

Perhaps Rebecca's greatest accomplishment is that she brings her husband's authority and her own understanding of the family's future together.

## The Price of Leadership

There is a personal cost to everything Rebecca accomplishes.

When she tells Jacob to flee to Laban, she expects him to be gone for a few days, until his brother's anger subsides. Instead, Jacob will spend twenty years away from home.

Rebecca remains behind with Isaac, Esau, and the daughters-in-law who have already caused her so much grief. The Torah never records a reunion between Rebecca and Jacob. We do not know whether she ever saw her beloved son again.

There is a profound sadness in that. Rebecca has helped secure the future of Abraham's family, but the immediate result is the loss of the daily presence of the son she loves.

And this brings us back to Esau and his bowl of lentils.

**Esau sacrifices the future to satisfy the present. Rebecca sacrifices the present to secure the future.**

I think that is the fundamental difference between them.

Esau possesses a birthright but does not appreciate its value. Rebecca, who does not possess the birthright herself, understands that an inheritance is not simply something we receive from the generation before us. It is something we are responsible for transmitting to the generation that follows us.

That responsibility is at the heart of leadership. Sometimes it means making decisions whose benefits we will never personally enjoy. Rebecca may not have anticipated the price she would pay, but she was willing to assume responsibility when she believed the future of her family was at stake.

Last week, Abraham sent his servant to find a wife for Isaac.

This week, we discover what Abraham found.

He found a woman who understood the importance of the covenant he had established and who would take responsibility for ensuring that it continued.

Perhaps Abraham was a better judge of leadership than we realized.

He hadn't simply found Isaac a wife.

**He had found the CEO of his family.**
