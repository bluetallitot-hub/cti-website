# With Great Power Comes Great Responsibility

*Parashat Lech Lecha*

What if the Israelites' enslavement in Egypt began not with Pharaoh's cruelty, but with Abram's failure to protect his own wife?

That sounds like an extraordinary accusation against the father of the Jewish people. But the accusation is not mine. It comes from Nachmanides—the Ramban—one of Judaism's great biblical commentators.

And to understand his criticism, we need to return to an earlier journey to Egypt, long before the Exodus.

## A Plan That Worked—for Abram

God calls Abram to leave his homeland and promises to make him a great nation, to bless him, and to make his name great. Almost immediately, however, a famine strikes the land, and Abram travels to Egypt with Sarai.

As they approach Egypt, Abram becomes afraid. Sarai is beautiful, and he worries that the Egyptians will kill him in order to take her. He therefore asks her to say that she is his sister.

His explanation is revealing:

**לְמַעַן יִיטַב־לִי בַעֲבוּרֵךְ**

*“So that it may go well with me because of you.”* — Genesis 12:13

Notice what Abram says. Not *so that we will be safe*, or *so that you will be protected*. **So that it may go well with me.**

And, at least initially, it does. Sarai is taken into Pharaoh's household, while Abram receives sheep, cattle, servants, and other wealth on her account.

The plan works. **For Abram.**

God then afflicts Pharaoh's household, and Pharaoh summons Abram to demand an explanation. Remarkably, it is Pharaoh who asks the moral question: “What is this you have done to me?” (Genesis 12:18).

Ramban does not excuse Abram's conduct. Commenting on Genesis 12:10, he writes:

**וְדַע כִּי אַבְרָהָם אָבִינוּ חָטָא חֵטְא גָּדוֹל בִּשְׁגָגָה**

*“Know that Abraham our father committed a great sin inadvertently.”*

Ramban faults Abram both for leaving the promised land rather than trusting in God's protection and for exposing Sarai to danger. He goes further, connecting Abram's conduct in Egypt with the exile that would later befall his descendants.

Whether or not we accept that connection, Ramban forces us to confront a difficult question: **What happens when someone entrusted with responsibility uses that position to protect himself at another person's expense?**

## The King and the Soldier

Abram is not the only biblical hero whose conduct raises this question.

Centuries later, King David sees Bathsheba, the wife of Uriah, one of his own warriors. David summons her and sleeps with her. When she becomes pregnant, he attempts to conceal what happened by bringing Uriah home from the battlefield, hoping that Uriah will spend the night with his wife.

But Uriah refuses. His fellow soldiers are still in the field, and the Ark is still with the army. How can he enjoy the comforts of home while they remain at war?

Uriah understands the responsibilities that come with his position. David, meanwhile, is trying to escape the consequences of his own actions.

When the concealment fails, David sends Uriah back to the battlefield carrying a letter to his commander, Joab. The letter orders Joab to place Uriah in the fiercest fighting and withdraw from him. Uriah is killed.

Abram's wrongdoing and David's are **not equivalent**. Abram exposed Sarai to foreseeable danger. David deliberately arranged Uriah's death. The difference in severity is enormous.

But there is a shared moral failure. Abram sought to protect himself while leaving Sarai vulnerable. David used his authority as king to sacrifice a man whose life he was responsible for protecting.

The contrast between David and Uriah is especially painful: **Uriah refuses to abandon his fellow soldiers, while David abandons Uriah.**

## What Is Authority For?

Jewish tradition offers a different understanding of responsibility.

Maimonides, in *Mishneh Torah, Hilchot Ishut* 15:19, teaches that a husband should **honor his wife more than himself and love her as himself**.

Imagine Abram approaching Egypt with that principle foremost in his mind. Instead of asking how Sarai might preserve *his* safety, he would have to ask what *he* owed her.

The same issue extends beyond marriage. When the Israelites demand a king, the prophet Samuel warns them about the danger of royal power (I Samuel 8). Their king may take their sons, their daughters, their fields, and their vineyards for his own purposes.

The word that dominates Samuel's warning is **take**.

The very person meant to lead and protect the people may instead use his authority to serve himself. David's treatment of Uriah gives that warning a terrible human face.

Authority is not simply a privilege. It creates obligations. And the greater the power entrusted to someone, the greater the responsibility to protect those who depend upon that person.

That principle applies to kings, but also to spouses, parents, teachers, employers, and community leaders. **Responsibility is not something that comes after authority. It is what gives authority its moral legitimacy.**

## Two Truths About Our Heroes

One of the most remarkable features of the Bible is its refusal to pretend that its heroes were perfect.

Abram becomes Abraham, the father of the Jewish people. David becomes Israel's great king, the figure whose dynasty remains central to Jewish messianic hope.

Yet the Bible preserves their failures. It tells us when they fell short of the very responsibilities their positions demanded.

**Their failures do not erase their greatness. But their greatness does not excuse their failures.**

Both things can be true.

Perhaps that is why these uncomfortable stories matter so much. We are not asked to admire great people uncritically, nor to deny everything good about them because they failed. We are asked to recognize that moral responsibility does not disappear with greatness. If anything, greatness makes that responsibility more urgent.

God promises Abram that he will become a blessing to the families of the earth. But being a blessing means more than receiving God's favor. It means becoming a source of blessing for the people whose lives are placed in our care.

And so, to quote the greatest rabbinical superhero of them all—Spider-Man:

**“With great power comes great responsibility.”**
