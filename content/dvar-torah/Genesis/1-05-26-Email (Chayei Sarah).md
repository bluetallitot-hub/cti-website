Have you ever tried to make a deal with God?

“God, if You help me get through this, I promise I'll be a better person.”

Or perhaps:

“God, if this is really what You want me to do, give me a sign.”

And not just any sign. Something unmistakable.

This week's parasha, Chayei Sarah, tells the story of Abraham sending his servant to find a wife for Isaac.

Abraham gives him specific instructions. The woman must come from Abraham's homeland and family. Isaac must remain in Canaan. And the woman must be willing to come.

Abraham assures him:

**הוּא יִשְׁלַח מַלְאָכוֹ לְפָנֶיךָ**

“He will send His angel before you.” (Genesis 24:7)

Abraham trusts that God will guide the mission.

But when the servant, traditionally identified as Eliezer, arrives at the well, he asks God for a sign.

He proposes that the woman who offers him water and volunteers to water his ten camels will be the woman God has chosen.

Now, that's not an unreasonable way to recognize kindness. Watering ten camels is no small favor!

But I think Eliezer makes a mistake.

**He takes a reasonable way of judging someone's character and turns it into a condition for recognizing God's will.**

Abraham had already assured him that God would guide the mission. Why did Eliezer need to prescribe how that guidance would appear?

And remarkably, the sign works.

Rebecca arrives, gives him water, and offers to water his camels.

Yet even after the sign is fulfilled, the Torah tells us Eliezer is still wondering whether God has made his journey successful (Genesis 24:21).

He still needs to investigate her family. He still needs to determine whether she's suitable. And he still needs to discover whether she's willing to go.

**The sign hasn't eliminated his responsibility, replaced his judgment, or guaranteed the outcome.**

The rabbis themselves debated Eliezer's behavior. The Talmud discusses his sign in connection with *nichush*, divination, while later commentators distinguish between arbitrary omens and meaningful tests of character.

But I think there's a deeper question.

Why did Eliezer need a sign at all?

There's a difference between asking God for guidance and telling God what that guidance must look like.

And then the Torah introduces a remarkable contrast.

Rebecca's family has already declared that the marriage comes from God.

**מֵה׳ יָצָא הַדָּבָר**

“The matter has come from the Lord.” (Genesis 24:50)

Yet Rebecca is still asked whether she will go.

**הֲתֵלְכִי עִם־הָאִישׁ הַזֶּה**

“Will you go with this man?” (Genesis 24:58)

She answers with a single word:

**אֵלֵךְ — “I will go.”**

Rebecca agrees to leave her home and family, travel to a distant land, and marry a man she has never met.

Does that sound familiar?

It's remarkably similar to Abraham's response when God called him to leave his homeland.

Both Abraham and Rebecca move toward a future they cannot yet see.

And neither demands certainty about the outcome.

**Abraham trusts. Eliezer seeks reassurance. Rebecca chooses.**

Perhaps that's what genuine faith looks like.

Not the absence of questions. Not certainty that everything will work out exactly as we hope.

And certainly not the expectation that God must provide a sign whenever we're uncertain.

There's nothing wrong with praying for healing, success, or guidance.

But there's a difference between praying for an outcome and making our faith depend upon receiving that outcome.

We have responsibilities. We must exercise our judgment. We must do what is right.

And we must recognize that there are things beyond our control.

**Faith isn't a contract in which we agree to trust God only if God delivers the outcome we've requested.**

We can ask. We can hope. We can even plead.

But we cannot make our trust conditional upon getting the answer we want.

Because faith isn't telling God how the road must unfold.

**Faith is doing our part, trusting God with what lies beyond our control, and having the courage to say, like Rebecca:**

**אֵלֵךְ — “I will go”**
