# Justice Through Hospitality

*Parashat Vayera*

Abraham is ninety-nine years old. He has just undergone circumcision. And according to rabbinic tradition, it is the third day after the procedure, when the pain is particularly intense.

At ninety-nine, three days after surgery, I think most of us would appreciate a little peace and quiet. A comfortable chair, perhaps a cup of tea, and absolutely nobody ringing the doorbell.

But Abraham apparently has other ideas.

The Talmud (Bava Metzia 86b) teaches that God made the day unusually hot so that travelers would stay off the roads and Abraham could recover in peace. Instead, Abraham was distressed because there were no guests to welcome.

So God sent three angels, appearing as travelers.

When Abraham sees them approaching, he runs to greet them. He offers water, shade, and something to eat. Then he involves Sarah and a servant in preparing an elaborate meal.

We traditionally understand this story as an example of *hachnasat orchim*, welcoming guests.

But I think something more is happening.

**Abraham isn't simply practicing hospitality. He's teaching justice.**

## A Household of Justice

A few verses later, God explains why Abraham was chosen:

**כִּי יְדַעְתִּיו לְמַעַן אֲשֶׁר יְצַוֶּה אֶת־בָּנָיו וְאֶת־בֵּיתוֹ אַחֲרָיו וְשָׁמְרוּ דֶּרֶךְ ה׳ לַעֲשׂוֹת צְדָקָה וּמִשְׁפָּט**

“For I have chosen him, so that he may instruct his children and his household after him to keep the way of the Lord by doing righteousness and justice.” (Genesis 18:19)

Notice what God says.

Abraham isn't chosen simply because he will be righteous. He's chosen because he will **teach righteousness**, not only to his children but to his entire household.

And perhaps the Torah has just shown us how.

Abraham doesn't gather everyone for a lecture about welcoming strangers. He sees people who need hospitality, and his household gets to work.

Sarah prepares the bread. A servant prepares the calf. Hospitality is simply how Abraham and Sarah's household operates.

Think about our own Shabbat tables.

When we invite guests, someone prepares the food, someone sets the table, and someone finds the extra chairs. Our children see it, our families participate, and everyone learns what matters by watching what we do.

**We don't always teach our values by explaining them. Sometimes we teach them by setting another place at the table.**

And this brings us back to God's description of Abraham's mission:

**צְדָקָה וּמִשְׁפָּט — righteousness and justice.**

We usually think of hospitality as *chesed*, loving-kindness. But the Torah's language suggests something more.

Kindness is something we choose to offer. Justice recognizes that another person has a claim upon us, whether or not we find it convenient.

When Abraham sees three strangers standing in the heat, he doesn't ask whether he knows them or whether they can repay him. He sees people whose needs matter.

Hospitality isn't simply an expression of Abraham's generosity. It's how he teaches his household to recognize the dignity of another human being.

And that lesson is about to become much more important.

## Abraham Demands Justice

A little later, God reveals that Sodom and Gomorrah face destruction because of their wickedness.

Abraham responds with one of the boldest questions in the Torah:

**הֲשֹׁפֵט כָּל־הָאָרֶץ לֹא יַעֲשֶׂה מִשְׁפָּט**

“Shall the Judge of all the earth not do justice?” (Genesis 18:25)

That's a rather extraordinary question to ask the Creator of the universe.

Abraham asks whether God would spare the city if fifty righteous people could be found there. God agrees. Then Abraham bargains down to forty-five, forty, thirty, twenty, and finally ten.

But beneath the bargaining is something important.

Abraham isn't denying Sodom's wickedness. He's insisting that innocent people must not be treated as guilty simply because of where they live.

And he isn't merely asking God to rescue his nephew Lot. He's asking God to spare the entire city for the sake of righteous people who might be living there.

People he may never have met, but whose lives matter.

At his tent, Abraham welcomed strangers because he recognized an obligation to care for them. Here, he defends strangers because he recognizes an obligation to protect the innocent.

**These aren't two unrelated acts. They're expressions of the same moral understanding: other people matter, even when they aren't part of our family or community.**

And this is what Abraham teaches his household—not simply by explaining justice, but by living according to it.

Yet how do we know whether those lessons take root?

The real test comes when someone leaves our household and finds themselves surrounded by people who don't share those values.

That's precisely what happens to Abraham's nephew Lot.

## A City That Cannot See

Lot traveled with Abraham from Haran and lived alongside Abraham and Sarah before eventually going his own way.

And where did he settle?

Sodom.

Not exactly the neighborhood one would choose for its strong moral values.

When two strangers arrive at the gates of Sodom, Lot rises to greet them, bows before them, and invites them into his home. He offers water, food, and a place to spend the night.

Sound familiar?

Just one chapter earlier, Abraham welcomed three strangers into his tent. Now Lot welcomes two strangers into his home.

I don't think that similarity is accidental.

The Torah doesn't explicitly tell us that Abraham taught Lot hospitality. But Lot spent years in Abraham's household, where welcoming strangers was part of everyday life.

And now he's practicing what he learned.

But there's a difference.

Abraham welcomed his guests in the relative safety of his own household. Lot is living in Sodom, where hospitality can be dangerous.

That evening, the men of the city surround Lot's house and demand that he surrender his guests. Lot goes outside and attempts to protect them.

Listen to what the people of Sodom say:

**הָאֶחָד בָּא־לָגוּר וַיִּשְׁפֹּט שָׁפוֹט**

“This one came here as a stranger, and now he presumes to judge!” (Genesis 19:9)

There's that word again.

**שפט —  judge**

Just one chapter earlier, Abraham challenged God:

“Shall the Judge of all the earth not do justice?”

**Abraham demands justice. The people of Sodom resent being judged.**

And I think that contrast tells us something profound.

The people of Sodom don't simply refuse to welcome strangers. They cannot understand why anyone would feel obligated to protect them.

To them, Lot's objection is itself an offense. Who is this outsider to tell us what is right and wrong?

Perhaps that is Sodom's deepest failure. Its inhabitants have lost the ability to see morally at all.

They cannot recognize the dignity of the strangers at Lot's door, the responsibility to protect them, or the legitimacy of someone who tells them that what they're doing is wrong.

But Lot remembers something.

He remembers that strangers deserve shelter and that welcoming someone into your home creates responsibilities.

And where might he have learned those lessons?

In the household of Abraham and Sarah.

Abraham couldn't have known that the values practiced around his table would one day be tested in a hostile city.

**Abraham's hospitality had become Lot's responsibility.**

## What Our Households Teach

When God explains why Abraham was chosen, God doesn't speak about his wealth or power. God speaks about the kind of household Abraham would build.

Abraham's greatness wasn't simply in the values he held. It was in his determination to pass those values on.

He did it by opening his tent, welcoming strangers, and involving his household in the work of hospitality.

We sometimes imagine that teaching our children Jewish values requires finding just the right words. But I suspect they learn at least as much from what we do when we're not trying to teach them anything.

They notice how we treat people, whether we're willing to inconvenience ourselves for someone in need, and whether the values we discuss are the same values we practice.

You can tell a child a hundred times that welcoming guests is a mitzvah.

But when that child grows up in a home where there's always room for one more person at the Shabbat table, perhaps you don't need to explain very much.

They already know.

And years later, they may find themselves setting another place at their own table, standing up for someone who needs protection, or refusing to remain silent in the face of injustice.

Not necessarily because they remember something we said.

But because they remember something we did.

We cannot control every environment our children will encounter. But we can help shape the values they carry with them.

Abraham thought he was welcoming three strangers for a meal. But he was doing something that would last far longer.

He was teaching his household how to see the world.

**Because the hospitality we practice today may become the justice someone else defends tomorrow.**
